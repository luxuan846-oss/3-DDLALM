Abstract
We develop a single-loop first-order framework for smooth nonconvex–concave minimax optimiza-
tion, with matching leading-order upper and lower bounds for optimization stationarity and game
stationarity in a projected zero-respecting oracle model. To establish the upper bound, we propose
a single-loop projected damped extragradient method, which couples projected extragradient
updates with dual momentum and a moving proximal center. Each iteration requires at most three
gradient evaluations, without the need to solve nested subproblems. For game stationarity, the
1/2
method requires a leading oracle complexity O(L3/2 DY ∆ϕ ε−5/2 ) for returning a deterministic
best-iterate whose first-order residual is below ε. Here, L is the gradient Lipschitz constant of
the objective function, DY bounds the diameter of the dual feasible set, and ∆ϕ is the value-
function gap that depends on the initial point. For optimization stationarity, the method requires
a leading oracle complexity of O(L2 DY ∆ϕ ε−3 ) for returning a uniformly selected iterate whose
expected squared Moreau-envelope gradient norm is at most ε2 . We also establish matching lower
1/2
bounds of Ω(L3/2 DY ∆ϕ ε−5/2 ) for game stationarity and Ω(L2 DY ∆ϕ ε−3 ) for optimization
stationarity over projected zero-respecting first-order methods. Under dual strong concavity, the

same framework achieves a leading first-order oracle complexity of O( κ L∆ϕ ε−2 ) under both
stationarity criteria, where κ is the dual condition number. Matching lower bounds hold for
both criteria in the same oracle model. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to
close the oracle complexity gap for smooth nonconvex–concave minimax optimization under both
stationarity criteria by simultaneously improving the upper and lower bounds in the projected
zero-respecting oracle model.

1 Introduction
We study the deterministic nonconvex–concave (NC–C) minimax problem
min max f (x, y ), (P)
x∈X y∈Y
where X is nonempty, closed, and convex, Y is nonempty, compact, and convex, and f has an L-
Lipschitz continuous gradient. The objective is possibly nonconvex in x and concave in y . Smooth
1
instances arise in distributionally robust learning [11], group robust learning [12], and learning with
average top-k losses [2]. In these applications, the dual variable weights samples or groups, or selects
large losses, while the primal variable parametrizes a possibly nonconvex model. An important
algorithmic question is whether optimal stationarity complexity can be attained by a single-loop
first-order method, without repeatedly solving auxiliary optimization problems.
We consider two stationarity criteria. Optimization stationarity (OS) measures the gradient of a
Moreau envelope of the value function ϕ(x) = maxy∈Y f (x, y ), extended by +∞ outside X . Game
stationarity (GS) measures the joint first-order residual of f . These criteria describe different aspects
of stationarity: OS concerns the minimization of the value function, whereas GS concerns the first-
order conditions of the minimax objective in both variables. They should also be distinguished from
the value-gradient and projected-gradient-mapping criteria used in earlier work. Formal definitions are
given in Section 2. Throughout this introduction, complexity refers to first-order oracle complexity.
Unless displayed explicitly, bounds suppress fixed smoothness, domain, and initialization quantities;
Oe additionally suppresses logarithmic factors.
Related work.
In the nonconvex–strongly concave (NC–SC) setting, let µ > 0 be the dual strong concavity parameter
and write κ = L/µ. For an unconstrained primal variable, two-timescale gradient descent ascent
(TS-GDA) attains O(κ2 ε−2 ) complexity for value-gradient stationarity [8, 9]. Under a dual Polyak–
Lojasiewicz condition on unconstrained domains, Smoothed-AGDA attains O(κε−2 ) complexity for
GS, with a terminal refinement giving the same leading order for value-gradient stationarity [16].
Minimax-PPA and Catalyst-EG/OGDA attain O e(√κε−2 ) complexity for value-gradient stationarity in
the corresponding unconstrained-primal settings√ under strong concavity [7, 19]. The single-loop mirror
descent ascent method MDA also reports O( κε−2 ) complexity √ under its prescribed mirror geometry
and mirror-gradient-mapping criterion [3]. Lower bounds of Ω( κL∆ϕ ε−2 ) are known for value-gradient
stationarity in the corresponding unconstrained oracle models [5, 19], where ∆ϕ = ϕ(x0 ) − inf x∈X ϕ(x).
The general NC–C setting is more difficult because the value function need not be smooth. Multi-
loop methods address this difficulty by solving regularized auxiliary problems. Prox-DIAG [13] and
Minimax-PPA [7] attain O e(ε−3 ) complexity for OS. Minimax-PPA also gives O e(ε−5/2 ) complexity
under its game-stationarity formulation. Li et al. [6] develop Perturbed Smoothed FOAM, which
attains Oe(ε−3 ) for OS and O e(ε−5/2 ) for GS. These methods use inner iterations to solve regularized
auxiliary problems.
Single-loop methods instead use a fixed number of elementary first-order updates per iteration, but
their representative NC–C guarantees have been weaker. TS-GDA has an O(ε−6 ) OS bound under an
additional primal Lipschitz assumption [8, 9]. AGP attains O(ε−4 ) under a projected primal–dual
gradient-mapping criterion on compact convex domains [15]. The analysis of Li et al. [6] establishes
O(ε−4 ) complexity for Smoothed GDA under both OS and GS. Their Perturbed Smoothed GDA
improves the GS bound to O(ε−3 ), while retaining an O(ε−4 ) OS bound. Thus, under both criteria, a
complexity gap remains between these single-loop methods and methods that rely on auxiliary solvers.
These comparisons require attention to the stationarity criterion and the reported output. In
particular, a small projected-gradient mapping does not in general imply a small normal-cone residual
at the same point. A conversion may require a projected output and an additional gradient evaluation.
We therefore distinguish the criteria stated in the original results and do not implicitly identify them
with the constrained residual used here.
Lower bounds provide a complementary perspective. Concurrent work establishes an Ω(ε−3 ) OS
lower bound for smooth NC–C problems: Wu et al. [14] consider zero-respecting first-order methods,
while Pan et al. [10] consider general deterministic first-order methods. Both results measure OS
through the Moreau-envelope gradient of the value function. For GS, the lower bounds in Li et al. [6]
concern specific GDA variants. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work establishes a matching
Ω(ε−5/2 ) lower bound for the class of projected zero-respecting first-order methods. Consequently, the
available GS upper bounds do not by themselves establish the optimal complexity.
These observations motivate the following question:
Can a single-loop first-order method attain optimal oracle complexity under both OS and GS, with matching
lower bounds certifying its optimality?
2
Our contributions.
We develop a single-loop framework that answers this question affirmatively within the projected
zero-respecting first-order oracle class. Here optimality concerns the leading complexity terms in the
stated accuracy ranges for fixed problem and initialization bounds, with the additive initialization
cost stated separately. The framework attains leading complexities O(ε−3 ) for OS and O(ε−5/2 ) for
GS without the need to solve nested subproblems or introduce multiplicative logarithmic factors in
these leading terms. Matching lower bounds establish its optimality under both criteria, including
what is, to our knowledge, the first matching problem-class lower bound for GS in the stated oracle
model. The framework also extends to dual strong concavity. Our contributions are detailed below,
where DY denotes an upper bound on the dual diameter.
1. We develop Moving-Center Extragradient with Momentum (MC-EGM), a projected damped
extragradient framework for smooth NC–C minimax optimization. The method combines projected
prediction–correction steps, dual momentum, and a moving proximal center, requiring at most three
gradient evaluations per iteration without the need to solve nested subproblems. Its analysis relies
on a new joint potential function that couples descent of a regularized envelope with tracking of its
saddle point. A suitably designed warm-up mechanism sharpens the initialization dependence using
the same elementary updates, without resetting the state or repeatedly solving inner subproblems.
2. For GS, the warm-started method requires a leading first-order oracle complexity of

factors from the leading term. We establish, to our knowledge, the first matching problem-class GS

Technical overview.
We introduce quadratic regularization to obtain a strongly convex–strongly concave saddle-point
problem. The primal regularization penalizes the squared distance from the primal variable to a
reference point, called the proximal center. Updating this center drives optimization progress but also
shifts the saddle point that the primal–dual iterates must track. The main challenge is to control the
resulting tracking error while updating the center at every iteration, without repeatedly solving the
regularized problem to a prescribed accuracy.
Our key observation is that the center update contributes a descent term to the estimate for the
regularized value function. This term helps absorb the additional tracking error caused by the same
update. We capture this relationship through a joint potential function that accounts for both the
regularized value and the tracking error. For a fixed center, projected extragradient updates with dual

The lower bounds use different mechanisms for the two criteria. The OS construction delays
successive primal coordinates through dual blocks and maintains a Moreau-gradient barrier before
the last coordinate is revealed. For GS, the barrier must hold at every feasible dual point, including
boundary points where normal vectors may cancel gradient components. The GS construction therefore
couples an alternating primal–dual chain with constraints for which a small residual forces the relevant
norm constraints to be inactive. The remaining sign constraints then allow the terminal signal to yield
a uniform residual barrier.

4 Complexity analysis
The analysis separates two effects of Algorithm 1: contraction of the primal–dual tracking error at
a fixed center and the perturbation caused by moving the center. We control these effects through
a joint potential function combining the regularized envelope with a residual-based tracking energy.
Its cross terms account for dual momentum and normal-cone feedback, without requiring an inner
stopping criterion.
4.1 Regularized saddle-point geometry
Recall G and p from (3.1)–(3.2). Throughout the next four lemmas, let rx > L, ry ≥ 0, and µ + ry > 0,
and set µx := rx − L, µy := µ + ry , and LG := L + rx + ry . Then G(·, ·; z ) is µx -strongly convex

5 Lower bounds
We establish lower bounds for OS and GS in both the NC–C and NC–SC settings under Assumption 2.1.
These bounds have the same parameter dependence as the leading terms in Theorem 3.1, within
the projected zero-respecting oracle model defined below and in the common accuracy ranges. The
comparison separates the additive initialization cost, as explained in Remark 3.1. The lower bounds
do not impose a single-loop restriction on competing algorithms.
5.1 Problem and algorithm classes
We first specify the problem class and oracle model. The problem class retains the smoothness, dual-
domain diameter, and initial value-gap parameters appearing in the upper bounds. The dimensions
are unrestricted and may depend on the target accuracy.
Definition 5.1 Fix L, DY , ∆ > 0 and 0 ≤ µ ≤ L. Let Fµ (L, DY , ∆) denote the class of minimax problems
satisfying Assumption 2.1, with joint gradient Lipschitz constant at most L, dual strong concavity parameter
at least µ, and diam(Y ) ≤ DY . The initial point is (0, 0) ∈ X × Y and satisfies ∆ϕ = ϕ(0) − inf x∈X ϕ(x) ≤ ∆.
When µ = 0, we write F(L, DY , ∆).
At each query point (x, y ), the first-order oracle returns (∇x f (x, y ), ∇y f (x, y )). We count joint
gradient evaluations and allow projections onto X and Y without charging additional oracle calls.
Following the zero-respecting framework [1, 5], we restrict each preprojection vector to coordinates
revealed by previous query points and oracle responses. We also record coordinates revealed by
intermediate projections, so that the model permits multiple internal projection stages between
gradient queries. This is a support restriction, rather than a linear-span restriction: the algorithm
may otherwise combine the available information arbitrarily.
Definition 5.2 (Projected zero-respecting algorithms) A first-order algorithm initialized at (0, 0) is projected
zero-respecting if its computation maintains revealed-coordinate sets Ix ⊆ {1, . . . , dx } and Iy ⊆ {1, . . . , dy },
initially empty, and satisfies the following rules.
(i) Every primal or dual vector formed before projection is supported on Ix or Iy , respectively. Within
these supports, its entries may be chosen arbitrarily using the available information.
(ii) The algorithm may evaluate ΠX (u) and ΠY (v) when supp(u) ⊆ Ix and supp(v) ⊆ Iy . The supports of
the resulting projection outputs are added to the corresponding revealed-coordinate sets.
(iii) Every gradient query is made at a feasible pair (x, y) with supp(x) ⊆ Ix and supp(y) ⊆ Iy . The supports
of the returned primal and dual gradients are then added to Ix and Iy , respectively.
(iv) Every reported output is feasible and obeys the same support restrictions, after any terminal projections
satisfying part (ii).
Only finitely many internal operations and projections are performed between successive gradient queries and
before an output is reported. For a randomized algorithm, all restrictions hold for every realization of its
internal randomness.
The initial gradient query at the origin is permitted because the zero vector has empty support.
Projection-generated normal vectors require no additional oracle: their supports are contained in
19
the union of the supports of the projection input and output. The definition does not permit an
independent normal-cone oracle that introduces unrevealed coordinates.
For OS, the output restriction applies to the reported primal point; for GS, it applies to both
components of the reported pair. The definition permits multiple internal stages, nested iterations, and
adaptive stopping rules. The model is coordinate dependent and is not the class of all deterministic or
randomized first-order algorithms.
Our constructions reveal coordinates successively through the first-order oracle. Until sufficiently
many coordinates have been revealed, the relevant OS or GS residual remains above the target accuracy.
The lower bounds therefore follow by controlling the number of coordinates that can be revealed per
oracle call. On the constructed feasible sets, projections preserve the relevant chain prefixes, although
a projection may reveal another coordinate within an already revealed group. Thus, arbitrarily many
permitted internal projections cannot advance the chain beyond the current prefix. This property is
imposed on the hard instances, not on the entire problem class.
Proposition 5.1 (Algorithm membership) On every problem in Fµ (L, DY , ∆), MC-EGM initialized with
x0 = y0 = z0 = 0 and ξ0 = n0 = v0 = 0 is projected zero-respecting in the sense of Definition 5.2. The same
statement holds with fixed-center warm-up and with either output rule in Algorithm 1.
Proof Proceed in the chronological order of gradient queries and internal projections. The initial vectors have
empty support. Suppose all stored primal and dual vectors are supported on the current revealed-coordinate
sets. The gradient of G is obtained from that of f by adding rx (x − z) in the primal block and subtracting
ry y in the dual block. These explicit corrections introduce no new coordinates.
The predictor and correction inputs are combinations of stored vectors and available gradients, so they
obey the preprojection support restrictions. Each projection output is added to the revealed coordinates before
it is used in a subsequent query. The normal vectors are combinations of projection inputs, outputs, and
available gradients. The momentum and center updates are likewise combinations of supported vectors. Thus,
the induction is preserved through every elementary operation.
Holding the center fixed changes none of these support properties, and retaining the warm-up state
preserves the induction. The GS output is a previously queried feasible pair. The OS output is a stored feasible
center supported on revealed primal coordinates. Selecting either output, deterministically or by a random
index, therefore satisfies the definition. □

norm constraints to be inactive, preventing their normal vectors from canceling the terminal signal.
The projections preserve prefixes of this order. For the p coordinates, this follows from the zero-tail
property of ΠCM . The coordinates qji with j ≥ 2 are unconstrained. For an unrevealed entrance group,
setting both vi and q1i to zero preserves feasibility and decreases the projection objective unless both
are already zero. Finally, projection onto Y preserves zero coordinates. Thus, projections cannot
bypass an unrevealed stage. These statements apply to every internal projection, so they remain valid
for any finite sequence of free projections between oracle calls.

6 Numerical experiments
We evaluate MC-EGM and its warm-up variant on two problems: a dense quartic minimax problem and
a fixed-candidate learning problem constructed from MNIST. The experiments examine whether the
proposed single-loop method translates its theoretical guarantees into competitive gradient complexity
and runtime.
We compare against four single-loop methods: GDA [8], AGP [15], Smoothed GDA [6, 17], and
Perturbed Smoothed GDA [6], as well as two nested methods: Perturbed Smoothed FOAM [4, 6] and
Minimax-PPA [7]. Convergence is measured by the constrained game-stationarity residual R(x, y ),
denoted by GS in the figures. Computational cost is reported in full-gradient oracle calls and elapsed
computation time; one oracle call returns both gradient blocks. For each problem, all methods use the
same initial point. Each method is evaluated using the best-performing configuration identified in its
respective parameter search.
The comparisons below are descriptive results for the reported instances and configurations. They do
not estimate worst-case complexity exponents, and they do not establish a uniform runtime advantage.
In particular, the experiments evaluate GS rather than the randomized Moreau-envelope OS guarantee.
The nonsmooth MNIST experiment is also outside the assumptions of the complexity analysis.

6.3 Scope of the numerical evidence
The two experiments illustrate the behavior of the single-loop framework and its one-time warm-
up, but they address only a limited part of the theoretical claims. The quartic example satisfies
the smooth NC–C assumptions on its compact domains. The MNIST example instead measures an
automatic-differentiation diagnostic for a small nonsmooth training problem. Neither experiment tests
the NC–SC condition-number dependence, the OS guarantee, or the worst-case accuracy exponents.
The comparisons also combine differences in update rules, parameter choices, and output mecha-
nisms. They should therefore be interpreted as performance of the tested implementations rather than
as an empirical ordering of the corresponding algorithm classes. A broader evaluation of parameter
sensitivity, independent initializations, and individual algorithmic components would be needed to
assess robustness of these observations.
7 Conclusions
This work closes the leading-order complexity gap between single-loop and nested first-order methods
for smooth nonconvex–concave minimax optimization under both optimization stationarity (OS)
and game stationarity (GS). The proposed MC-EGM combines projected extragradient steps, dual
momentum, and proximal-center updates within a single loop, without the need to solve nested
auxiliary problems. A unified Lyapunov analysis controls optimization progress and saddle-point
tracking, while a one-time fixed-center warm-up makes the leading complexity terms depend on ∆ϕ
rather than ∆0 , with only a lower-order initialization cost.
In the nonconvex–concave setting, the resulting leading oracle complexities are O(L2 DY ∆ϕ ε−3 )
1/2
for OS and O(L3/2 DY ∆ϕ ε−5/2 ) for GS. Thus, a single-loop method attains both rates without a
logarithmic
√ factor in the leading terms. Under dual strong concavity, the leading complexity improves
to O( κ L∆ϕ ε−2 ) for both criteria. These statements separate the additive initialization cost and
describe the leading behavior for fixed problem and initialization quantities with ∆ϕ > 0 as ε ↓ 0.
The end-to-end theorem provides budgets based on supplied upper bounds, a randomized OS output
satisfying an expected squared Moreau-gradient guarantee, and a deterministic GS output selected by
a computable certificate.
Matching lower bounds establish the optimality of all four leading complexity orders within the
projected zero-respecting oracle model. In particular, the constructions certify the dependence on
the displayed problem parameters, rather than only the accuracy exponents. The lower bounds allow
competing methods with nested iterations or additional internal stages, as well as randomized methods
satisfying the support restrictions for every realization. The randomized output comparison is made
33
at a fixed query budget. The model explicitly permits free internal projections, and the algorithm-
membership result places the zero-initialized MC-EGM framework within this model. The optimality
claim is therefore not restricted to single-loop competitors, although it remains specific to the stated
oracle model and accuracy ranges.
Experiments on a dense quartic minimax problem and a finite-candidate MNIST training problem
complement the theory. MC-EGM reaches the prescribed residual thresholds with fewer gradient calls
and lower measured runtime than the tested baselines, and the one-time warm-up further reduces
both costs. The MNIST experiment also illustrates empirical performance beyond the smooth setting
of the analysis, without extending the theoretical guarantees to nonsmooth networks.
Together, these results show that optimal leading-order oracle complexity for both OS and GS can
be attained through coordinated single-loop updates rather than nested solvers. Natural directions for
future work include stochastic extensions, unbounded dual domains, and lower bounds for broader
first-order oracle classes. A further question is whether the complete initialization dependence can be
characterized or improved under the same basic problem assumptions.
