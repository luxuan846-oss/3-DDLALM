Efficient Parameter-Free First-Order Methods for Nonsmooth Composite Minimax Optimization

In this paper we propose first-order methods for a class of nonsmooth composite strongly convex–strongly concave and nonconvex–concave minimax optimization. We first develop an inexact proximal method and an accumulative regularizated method for strongly convex–strongly concave problems. The latter achieves the optimal dependence on the curvature parameters and places the smaller curvature inside the accuracy logarithm. Using this method as a subsolver, we propose a proximal point method for nonconvex–concave problems. Under suitable assumptions, it finds an $\epsilon$ -stationary point with an operation complexity of $O(\epsilon^{-5/2})$ , which improves the best-known $O(\epsilon^{-5/2}\log(1/\epsilon))$ bounds by removing the logarithmic factor. We further develop parameter-free variants for both problem classes, and achieve the same complexity without knowledge of any problem constants. All proposed methods are equipped with verifiable termination criteria.

Keywords: Minimax optimization, parameter free, first-order methods, accelerated proximal methods, accumulated regularization, operation complexity.

Mathematics Subject Classification: 90C47, 90C26, 90C25, 65K05.

1 Introduction

In this paper, we consider the composite minimax problem

$\min_{x}\max_{y}\{H(x,y):=h(x,y)+p(x)-q(y)\}.$ (1)

Throughout the paper, we let $\mathcal{X}:=\dom p$ and $\mathcal{Y}:=\dom q$ . We make the following assumptions.

$p:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to(-\infty,+\infty]$ and $q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,+\infty]$ are proper closed convex functions, and the proximal operator of $p$ and $q$ can be exactly evaluated.

The set $\mathcal{Y}$ is bounded, namely, we have $\|y\|\leq D_{\mathbf{y}}$ for some $D_{\mathbf{y}}\geq 1$ .

$h:\mathbb{R}^{m+n}\to\mathbb{R}$ is $L_{\nabla h}$ -smooth on $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ for some $L_{\nabla h}>0$ . For any $x\in\mathcal{X}$ , $h(x,\cdot)$ is concave on $\mathcal{Y}$ .

Problem ( 1 ) has at least one optimal solution with finite optimal value $H^{*}=\min_{x}\max_{y}H(x,y)$ .

Problem ( 1 ) provides a common formulation for several learning and decision problems. Examples include distributionally robust learning [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] and learning with nondecomposable losses, including area-under-the-curve maximization [ 3 , 4 ] . In these applications, the minimization variable represents a prediction model, while the maximization variable describes an uncertain distribution or arises from a variational representation of the loss. The convex terms $p$ and $q$ incorporate constraints and nonsmooth regularization. Problems of this form also arise as subproblems in methods for constrained minimax optimization [ 5 ] . Their scale and composite structure motivate methods based on evaluations of $h$ , $\nabla h$ , and the proximal operators of $p$ and $q$ .

There have been numerous studies on structured special cases of ( 1 ). When $h(\cdot,y)$ is convex for every $y\in\mathcal{Y}$ , its saddle-point optimality conditions form a monotone inclusion (MI). More generally, consider finding $u$ such that $0\in(F+B)(u)$ , where $F$ is monotone and $L_{F}$ -Lipschitz continuous, and $B$ is maximally monotone with an exactly computable resolvent. Proximal-point, splitting, extragradient, and extrapolation methods have been extensively studied for monotone inclusions and their saddle-point special cases [ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 ] . More recent work establishes accelerated pointwise residual guarantees through anchoring and related extrapolation schemes [ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 ] . Under $\mu$ -strong monotonicity, an $O((L_{F}/\mu)\log(1/\epsilon))$ bound, with the remaining problem data fixed, can be obtained for the numbers of evaluations of $F$ and the resolvent of $B$ , respectively, to find an $\epsilon$ -residual solution [ 11 ] . Such methods apply to strongly convex–strongly concave (SCSC) minimax problems, but treating these problems as general strongly monotone inclusions only uses the smaller of their two curvature parameters.

More specifically, consider an SCSC problem with the same composite structure as ( 1 ), with $h$ replaced by an $L_{\nabla f}$ -smooth function $f$ that is $\mu_{x}$ -strongly convex in $x$ and $\mu_{y}$ -strongly concave in $y$ , where $0<\mu_{y}\leq\mu_{x}\leq L_{\nabla f}$ . Neither $\mathcal{X}$ nor $\mathcal{Y}$ needs to be bounded. A direct application of the above MI complexity has a dependence on $L_{\nabla f}/\mu_{y}$ . Accelerated methods exploit the separate primal and dual curvatures to improve this dependence [ 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] , while lower bounds characterize the attainable dependence on the smoothness and curvature parameters [ 20 ] . In particular, the method in [ 19 ] attains the optimal factor $L_{\nabla f}/\sqrt{\mu_{x}\mu_{y}}$ by combining a pointwise-conjugate reformulation with acceleration and an MI subsolver. A modification with a verifiable termination criterion for approximate primal-dual stationarity was developed in [ 5 ] . Nevertheless, when SCSC problems serve as subproblems and $\mu_{y}$ decreases with the target accuracy, the scale inside the accuracy logarithm also matters.

We address this issue with two SCSC methods. The first applies an accelerated inexact proximal scheme to a strongly convex reformulation of the saddle problem. It builds on the reformulation in [ 19 ] , proximal acceleration [ 21 , 22 , 23 ] , and relative-error criteria for inexact proximal methods [ 24 , 25 , 26 ] . The resulting subproblems have comparable primal and dual curvatures and can be solved using an MI subsolver that returns a computed pointwise residual. Our analysis requires only a residual bound proportional to $L_{F}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|/N$ , where $N$ bounds the separate evaluation counts. The second method combines this contraction with accumulated regularization. This approach is related to recursive regularization for gradient minimization [ 27 ] and, more directly, the accumulated-regularization framework in [ 28 ] . By increasing the regularization and updating its center to retain the preceding quadratic terms, we obtain a bound with the smaller curvature $\mu_{y}$ inside the accuracy logarithm. This improvement is essential for the nonconvex–concave application.

When $h(\cdot,y)$ is nonconvex, most existing methods aim to find an approximate stationary point. This setting has been studied through multistep and two-timescale gradient descent-ascent [ 29 , 30 ] , proximal-point methods [ 3 , 16 , 31 , 32 , 33 ] , and smoothing methods [ 34 , 35 , 36 ] . Alternating proximal and projected-gradient methods have also been developed for composite and constrained formulations [ 37 , 38 ] . The intermediate nonconvex–strongly concave setting has also received substantial attention, including near-optimal algorithms and lower bounds [ 16 , 39 ] . Two commonly studied criteria in the nonconvex–concave setting are stationarity of the primal value function $\max_{y}H(\cdot,y)$ , measured through its Moreau envelope, and approximate satisfaction of the first-order conditions in both variables. The latter is the $\epsilon$ -stationarity criterion used in this paper and is defined in Subsection 1.1. These criteria and the oracles used to attain them must be distinguished when comparing complexity bounds.

For stationarity of the primal value function, the method in [ 16 ] achieves an $O(\epsilon^{-3}\log^{2}(1/\epsilon))$ complexity for smooth constrained problems. For stationarity in both variables, the accelerated inexact proximal point smoothing method in [ 32 ] achieves an $O(\epsilon^{-5/2})$ bound under an oracle that exactly solves a regularized maximization problem involving the coupling function. Such an oracle generally requires more than a gradient or a proximal evaluation. Using gradients and the separate proximal operators, an $O(\epsilon^{-5/2}\log(1/\epsilon))$ bound was established in [ 5 ] ; see also [ 36 ] for a smoothing and perturbation approach with the same dependence on $\epsilon$ for game stationarity. Removing this logarithmic factor while retaining computable stopping criteria is the main objective of our nonconvex analysis.

We propose an inexact proximal point method that uses the new SCSC solver to attain this objective. The smaller-curvature residual bound allows the total inner cost to be controlled by the descent over the outer iterations. Consequently, the method finds an $\epsilon$ -stationary point with an operation complexity $O(\epsilon^{-5/2})$ . To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to remove the logarithmic factor for the general problem class in ( 1 ). Independent and concurrent work [ 40 ] also removes this factor for a special case of ( 1 ) using a substantially different approach. Moreover, our framework accommodates proximable nonsmooth terms and admits a parameter-free variant that achieves the same complexity bound.

Implementing minimax methods often requires smoothness and curvature parameters or a bound on the dual domain. Adaptive methods have been studied for monotone inclusions and convex optimization [ 11 , 12 , 28 , 41 ] . Methods that adapt to unknown smoothness and strong concavity parameters have also been developed for nonconvex–strongly concave problems [ 42 , 43 ] , and parameter-free line-search methods have been proposed for the nonconvex–concave setting [ 44 ] . We develop parameter-free variants for both problem classes considered here. The SCSC method accepts tentative parameters through a computed residual-reduction test. The nonconvex–concave method adapts its smoothness estimate through a verifiable model inequality, and its parameter-free variant also selects a dual-radius estimate. Their complexity bounds count unsuccessful trials as well as successful ones. The main contributions are summarized below.

We develop an accelerated inexact proximal method that reduces the solution distance by a fixed factor within $O(L_{\nabla f}/\sqrt{\mu_{x}\mu_{y}})$ evaluations of $\nabla f$ , $\prox_{p}$ , and $\prox_{q}$ , respectively. Using it as a subsolver, our accumulative regularization method finds an $\epsilon$ -stationary point within $O((L_{\nabla f}/\sqrt{\mu_{x}\mu_{y}})\log(1/\epsilon))$ evaluations of each oracle. This bound retains the optimal dependence on the curvature parameters and places the smaller curvature $\mu_{y}$ inside the accuracy logarithm, which is essential for removing the logarithmic factor in the nonconvex–concave complexity. We also propose a parameter-free variant that preserves the optimal complexity for SCSC minimax problem. All proposed algorithms are equipped with verifiable termination criteria.

We develop a proximal point method that finds an $\epsilon$ -stationary point of ( 1 ) within $O(\epsilon^{-5/2})$ evaluations of $h$ , $\nabla h$ , $\prox_{p}$ , and $\prox_{q}$ , respectively, for fixed problem data. To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to remove the logarithmic factor from the best-known $O(\epsilon^{-5/2}\log(1/\epsilon))$ complexity bound for the general problem class ( 1 ), while the independent and concurrent work [ 40 ] obtains the same improvement for a special case of ( 1 ) using a substantially different approach. Moreover, our method is parameter-free and equipped with a verifiable termination criterion.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows. In Subsection 1.1, we introduce some notation and terminology. In Section 2, we propose first-order methods for SCSC minimax optimization and study their complexity. In Section 3, we propose a first-order method for problem ( 1 ) and its parameter-free variant, and present complexity results for them. Finally, we provide the proofs of the main results in Section 4.

1.1 Notation and preliminaries

The following notation will be used throughout this paper. Let $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ denote the Euclidean space of dimension $d$ , $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$ denote the standard inner product, and $\|\cdot\|$ denote the Euclidean norm. We write $\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\ldots\}$ . For any $a\in\mathbb{R}$ , let $a_{+}=\max\{a,0\}$ , and let $\lceil a\rceil$ and $\lfloor a\rfloor$ denote the least integer greater than or equal to $a$ and the greatest integer less than or equal to $a$ , respectively. The notation $\log$ denotes the natural logarithm, and $\log_{b}$ denotes the logarithm to base $b$ . Given a point $u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ and a set $S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , let $\mathrm{dist}(u,S)=\inf_{v\in S}\|u-v\|$ , with $\mathrm{dist}(u,\varnothing)=+\infty$ .

Given a proper closed convex function $g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to(-\infty,+\infty]$ , its domain is $\dom g=\{u:g(u)<+\infty\}$ , and its subdifferential is

$\partial g(u)=\{v:g(z)\geq g(u)+\langle v,z-u\rangle\ \text{for all }z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}\qquad\forall u\in\dom g,$

with $\partial g(u)=\varnothing$ for $u\notin\dom g$ . The proximal operator associated with $g$ is denoted by $\prox_{g}$ and defined as

$\prox_{g}(u)=\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{g(z)+\frac{1}{2}\|z-u\|^{2}\right\}\qquad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.$

We count the evaluation of $\prox_{\gamma g}(u)$ as one evaluation of the proximal operator of $g$ for any $\gamma>0$ and $u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ .

A mapping $G$ is said to be $L_{G}$ -Lipschitz continuous on a set $S$ if $\|G(u)-G(v)\|\leq L_{G}\|u-v\|$ for all $u,v\in S$ . A function $g$ is said to be $L_{\nabla g}$ -smooth on $S$ if $\nabla g$ exists on $S$ and is $L_{\nabla g}$ -Lipschitz continuous there. A function $g$ is $\mu$ -strongly convex on a convex set $S$ if $g-\mu\|\cdot\|^{2}/2$ is convex on $S$ , and $L$ -weakly convex on $S$ if $g+L\|\cdot\|^{2}/2$ is convex on $S$ . It is $\mu$ -strongly concave if $-g$ is $\mu$ -strongly convex.

For $\epsilon\geq 0$ , a point $(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ is called an $\epsilon$ -stationary point of the minimax problem ( 1 ) if

$\mathrm{dist}(0,\nabla_{x}h(x,y)+\partial p(x))\leq\epsilon,\qquad\mathrm{dist}(0,\nabla_{y}h(x,y)-\partial q(y))\leq\epsilon.$

We use the same definition for other composite minimax problems, with $h$ replaced by the corresponding smooth coupling.

For a set-valued operator $T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , let $\dom T=\{u:T(u)\neq\varnothing\}$ . The operator $T$ is $\mu$ -strongly monotone if

$\langle v-w,u-z\rangle\geq\mu\|u-z\|^{2}\qquad\forall u,z\in\dom T,\ v\in T(u),\ w\in T(z),$

and monotone if the same inequality holds with $\mu=0$ . It is maximally monotone if its graph is not properly contained in the graph of another monotone operator. Let $I$ denote the identity operator. For maximally monotone $T$ and $\gamma>0$ , the resolvent $(I+\gamma T)^{-1}$ is single-valued, defined on $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , and nonexpansive, that is, $1$ -Lipschitz continuous. We count the evaluation of $(I+\gamma T)^{-1}(u)$ as one evaluation of the resolvent of $T$ for any $\gamma>0$ and $u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ .

Define

$A(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}h(x,y)\\ -\nabla_{y}h(x,y)\end{pmatrix},\qquad B(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}\partial p(x)\\ \partial q(y)\end{pmatrix}.$

The KKT system of ( 1 ) is $0\in(A+B)(u)$ . By Assumption 1 (iii), $A$ is $L_{\nabla h}$ -Lipschitz continuous on $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ , and $A+L_{\nabla h}I$ is monotone there. By Assumption 1 (i), $B$ is maximally monotone, and for every $\gamma>0$ ,

$(I+\gamma B)^{-1}(x,y)=(\prox_{\gamma p}(x),\prox_{\gamma q}(y)).$

2 First-order methods for strongly convex–strongly concave minimax optimization

In this section we consider the strongly convex–strongly concave minimax problem

$\min_{x}\max_{y}\{f(x,y)+p(x)-q(y)\},$ (2)

where $p:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to(-\infty,+\infty]$ and $q:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,+\infty]$ are proper, closed, convex, and proximable. No boundedness assumption is imposed on their domains. We make the following assumption on $f$ .

The function $f$ is $\mu_{x}$ -strongly convex in $x$ , $\mu_{y}$ -strongly concave in $y$ , and $L_{\nabla f}$ -smooth on $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ , where $0<\mu_{y}\leq\mu_{x}\leq L_{\nabla f}$ .

The ordering $\mu_{y}\leq\mu_{x}$ entails no loss of generality. If $\mu_{x}<\mu_{y}$ , exchanging the two variable blocks and reversing the objective sign yields the equivalent saddle-point with the curvature bounds and proximal mappings interchanged.

Under Assumption 2 , problem ( 2 ) has a unique saddle point. For $u=(x,y)$ , set

$F(u)=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{x}f(x,y)\\ -\nabla_{y}f(x,y)\end{pmatrix},\qquad B(u)=\begin{pmatrix}\partial p(x)\\ \partial q(y)\end{pmatrix}.$ (3)

The KKT system of ( 2 ) is $0\in(F+B)(u)$ . Under Assumption 2 , $F$ is $L_{\nabla f}$ -Lipschitz and $\mu_{y}$ -strongly monotone on $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ . Moreover, $B$ is maximally monotone, and its resolvent is evaluated by the proximal operators of $p$ and $q$ .

More generally, consider monotone inclusion $0\in(F+B)(u)$ , where $B$ is maximally monotone with an exactly computable resolvent, $F$ is monotone and $L_{F}$ -Lipschitz on $\dom B$ , and $F+B$ is $\mu$ -strongly monotone on $\dom B$ , with $0<\mu\leq L_{F}$ . Suppose this inclusion has a solution $u^{*}$ , which is unique. Although linearly convergent methods are available for strongly monotone inclusions [ 11 ] , a sublinear pointwise residual bound of order $L_{F}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|/(k+1)$ suffices for our analysis. We therefore impose the following requirement on the subsolver.

There exist an algorithm $\mathcal{A}(F,B,u^{0},L_{F},\mu)$ and constants $\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{A}}>0$ and $N_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{N}$ , with $N_{\mathcal{A}}\geq 1$ , such that the following holds. From any $u^{0}\in\dom B$ , one run of $\mathcal{A}$ generates, for every $\vartheta>0$ , a computed pair $(u,r)$ with

$r\in(F+B)(u),\qquad\|r\|\leq\vartheta\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|,$

using at most $N_{\mathcal{A}}+\lceil\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{A}}L_{F}/\vartheta\rceil$ evaluations of $F$ and of the resolvent of $B$ , respectively. Every evaluation of $F$ is at a point of $\dom B$ . Both counts include initialization and computation of $r$ . The constants depend only on $\mathcal{A}$ , not on the inclusion, its parameters, or its initial point. Neither $u^{*}$ nor its distance from $u^{0}$ is an input.

For the subdifferential operator $B$ used here, the EAG and past-EAG methods in [ 45 , Corollary 3.1] , with one forward–backward initialization step, satisfy Assumption 3 . With $\tau_{k}=1/(k+2)$ , one may take $(N_{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{A}})=(4,22)$ for EAG with stepsize $1/(2L_{F})$ , and $(N_{\mathcal{A}},\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{A}})=(4,28)$ for past-EAG with stepsize $1/(3L_{F})$ .

2.1 An inexact proximal method for SCSC problems

We first develop an accelerated inexact proximal method for the SCSC problem ( 2 ). Our approach applies the accelerated proximal point framework of [ 25 ] to a strongly convex reformulation of the saddle-point problem. Specifically, we consider

$\min_{\xi,y}\Phi(\xi,y)\quad\mbox{where}\quad\Phi(\xi,y)=q(y)+\sup_{x}\left\{\frac{\mu_{x}}{2}\|x-\xi\|^{2}-f(x,y)-p(x)\right\}\qquad\forall y\in\mathcal{Y},$ (4)

with $\Phi(\xi,y)=+\infty$ for $y\notin\mathcal{Y}$ . The function $\Phi$ is proper, closed, and $\mu_{y}$ -strongly convex, and its unique minimizer coincides with the saddle point $u^{*}$ of ( 2 ). The key to implementing this approach is that the proximal step

$\min_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{m+n}}\left\{\Phi(v)+\frac{\mu_{x}}{2}\|v-c^{k}\|^{2}\right\}$

can be computed through the balanced saddle problem

$\min_{x}\max_{y}\left\{f(x,y)+p(x)-q(y)-\frac{\mu_{x}}{4}\|x-c_{x}^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\mu_{x}}{2}\|y-c_{y}^{k}\|^{2}\right\}.$ (5)

For $\mu_{y}\leq\mu_{x}$ , the quadratic terms balance the primal and dual curvatures at order $\mu_{x}$ . Moreover, an approximate KKT pair for this problem yields, after a simple correction, an inexact proximal certificate for $\Phi$ ; see Lemma 2 .

We control the accuracy of the inner solves through a relative-error criterion with an additional absolute allowance, based on the mixed-error setting studied in [ 26 ] . Combining these inexact proximal steps with residual-corrected extrapolation yields Algorithm 1 below. The algorithm requires neither evaluating $\Phi$ nor computing the supremum defining it.

2.2 An accumulative regularizated method for SCSC problems

In this subsection we use Algorithm 1 to find an $\epsilon$ -stationary point of ( 2 ), where $\epsilon>0$ is given. Algorithm 1 reduces the initial solution distance by an absolute factor. Its residual bound, however, involves $\mu_{x}$ . We combine these contractions with accumulated regularization to obtain an evaluation bound involving the smaller curvature $\mu_{y}$ inside the accuracy logarithm.

At each regularized iteration we approximately solve a saddle problem with center $\bar{u}=(\bar{u}_{x},\bar{u}_{y})$ of the form

$\min_{x}\max_{y}\left\{f(x,y)+p(x)-q(y)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x-\bar{u}_{x}\|^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|y-\bar{u}_{y}\|^{2}\right\},\qquad\lambda>0.$

Following the accumulated-center construction in [ 28 , Algorithm 2.1] , we increase $\lambda$ and update $\bar{u}$ so that the preceding quadratic terms are retained. One preliminary call to Algorithm 1 bounds the remaining solution distance by its computed displacement. We use this displacement and $\epsilon$ to choose the initial regularization, and then continue the accumulation until the original residual is at most $\epsilon$ .

$f_{k}(x,y)=f(x,y)+\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\|x-\bar{u}_{x}^{k}\|^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}\|y-\bar{u}_{y}^{k}\|^{2}.$

The preliminary call determines $\lambda_{1}$ from the computed displacement. At each regularized iteration, $r^{k+1}$ is the residual of the regularized subproblem, and step 4 converts it to the original residual $e^{k+1}$ used in the stopping test.

2.3 A parameter-free method for SCSC problems

In this subsection we develop a variant of Algorithm 2 for solving ( 2 ) without knowing $L_{\nabla f}$ , $\mu_{x}$ , or $\mu_{y}$ . We call Algorithm 2 with tentative smoothness and curvature parameters and accept its output when the residual is sufficiently reduced.

In addition to Assumption 3 , we require an implementation of $\mathcal{A}$ that is well-defined for all positive supplied parameters and can be interrupted before an evaluation of $F$ or the resolvent of $B$ . These requirements apply to every supplied inclusion, even when its smoothness and curvature parameters are invalid. No convergence guarantee is required for invalid parameters.

For the simplicity of our discussion, let $\mathcal{I}$ be the list of integer triples $(i,j,k)$ with $i\geq 0$ and $0\leq j\leq k$ , ordered by increasing $2i+j+k$ , with any fixed order for ties. Each call to Algorithm 2 below is called a trial. Its evaluation limit applies separately to the numbers of evaluations of $\nabla f$ , $\prox_{p}$ , and $\prox_{q}$ , including all inner calls, initializations, and residual computations.

3 First-order method for nonconvex–concave minimax optimization

3.1 A proximal point method for NCC problems without knowledge of $L_{\nabla L}$

We first develop a method for ( 1 ) that uses a supplied radius $D\geq 1$ and calls Algorithm 2 without knowing $L_{\nabla h}$ . By Assumption 1 (ii), $\|y-y^{0}\|\leq 2D_{\mathbf{y}}$ for every $y\in\mathcal{Y}$ . Thus, with $D=2D_{\mathbf{y}}$ , the method finds an $\epsilon$ -stationary point of ( 1 ). We impose the additional execution requirements on $\mathcal{A}$ stated in Subsection 2.3 , including when the supplied subproblems are not SCSC.

Each call to Algorithm 2 is called a trial. The dual regularization parameter $\mu$ remains fixed. We maintain a primal center $x^{t}$ , an initial pair $(\hat{x},\hat{y})$ for the inner call, and a residual $r^{t}$ of the subproblem ( 16 ) at that pair. After an accepted trial, the returned primal point becomes the next center and the returned pair initializes the next call. A rejected trial retains the center and initial pair and doubles $\beta_{t}$ . A trial is called accepted if its call returns, passes the radius test in step 5, fails the stopping test in step 6, and satisfies ( 18 ). The radius test returns failure when the supplied radius is exceeded and Algorithm 5 uses this outcome to select the radius.

$\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mu_{x}}\left(\Phi(\tilde{u}^{1})-\Phi(u^{*})\right)+\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{u}^{1}-u^{*}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{x}}\langle(-e_{x}^{1},e_{y}^{1}),\tilde{u}^{1}-c^{0}\rangle+\frac{1}{2\mu_{x}^{2}}\|e^{1}\|^{2}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\mu_{x}^{2}}\left(\left\|\mu_{x}(\tilde{u}^{1}-c^{0})+(-e_{x}^{1},e_{y}^{1})\right\|^{2}-\mu_{x}^{2}\|\tilde{u}^{1}-c^{0}\|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{64}-1\right)\|\tilde{u}^{1}-c^{0}\|^{2}\leq-\frac{31}{64}\|\tilde{u}^{1}-c^{0}\|^{2},$

where the first inequality follows from $\bar{u}^{0}=c^{0}=u^{0}$ and ( 33 ), and the second inequality follows from ( 22 ) and $c^{0}=u^{0}$ . Summing this inequality and ( 35 ) for $i=1,\ldots,k$ yields ( 30 ). ∎

The following lemma bounds the accumulated error in ( 30 ).

Suppose Assumptions 2 and 3 hold. Let $u^{*}$ be the unique saddle point of ( 2 ), $\Phi$ be defined in ( 4 ), and $\bar{u}^{i}$ be defined by ( 29 ). Then the sequence generated by Algorithm 1 satisfies $\|c^{k}-u^{0}\|\leq 3\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|$ and

$\frac{(k+2)^{2}}{4\mu_{x}}\bigl(\Phi(\tilde{u}^{k+1})-\Phi(u^{*})\bigr)+\frac{1}{2}\|\bar{u}^{k+1}-u^{*}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{16}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(i+2)^{2}\|\tilde{u}^{i+1}-c^{i}\|^{2}\leq\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|^{2}$ (36)

$\|\tilde{u}^{i}-u^{*}\|\leq 2\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|,\qquad\|\bar{u}^{i}-u^{*}\|\leq\sqrt{2}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|\qquad\forall 0\leq i\leq k+1.$ (37)

$\displaystyle\|c^{i}-u^{0}\|=\left\|\frac{i}{i+2}(\tilde{u}^{i}-u^{*})+\frac{2}{i+2}(\bar{u}^{i}-u^{*})+(u^{*}-u^{0})\right\|$ (38) $\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2i+2\sqrt{2}}{i+2}+1\right)\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|\leq 3\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|,$

$\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}^{t+1}-u^{*}\|=\left\|\frac{t}{t+2}(\tilde{u}^{t}-u^{*})+\frac{2}{t+2}(\bar{u}^{t+1}-u^{*})+\frac{1}{\mu_{x}}(-2r_{x}^{t+1},r_{y}^{t+1})\right\|$ $\displaystyle\leq\frac{t}{t+2}\|\tilde{u}^{t}-u^{*}\|+\frac{2}{t+2}\|\bar{u}^{t+1}-u^{*}\|+\frac{1}{8}\left(\|\tilde{u}^{t+1}-c^{t}\|+\frac{\|c^{t}-u^{0}\|}{(t+2)^{2}}\right)$ $\displaystyle\leq\frac{2t+2\sqrt{2}+3/4}{t+2}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|\leq 2\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|,$

where the first inequality follows from ( 22 ) and ( 27 ), and the second follows from ( 38 ), ( 39 ), the induction hypothesis, and $t+2\geq 2$ . This completes the induction and establishes ( 36 ). Finally, by ( 29 ) and ( 37 ), one has

$\|c^{k}-u^{0}\|\leq\frac{k}{k+2}\|\tilde{u}^{k}-u^{*}\|+\frac{2}{k+2}\|\bar{u}^{k}-u^{*}\|+\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|\leq 3\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|.$ (40)

We can now establish the point and residual bounds.

Suppose Assumptions 2 and 3 hold. Let $u^{*}$ be the unique saddle point of ( 2 ), and let $e^{k+1}$ and $\tilde{u}^{k+1}$ be defined by ( 8 ) and ( 9 ), respectively, in Algorithm 1 . Then, at every reached iteration $k$ , we have

The next lemma bounds the residual $e^{k+1}$ defined in step 4 of Algorithm 2 .

Suppose that Assumptions 2 and 3 hold. Let $u^{*}$ be the unique saddle point of ( 2 ). For the sequence generated by Algorithm 2 , let $u^{1}$ and $\lambda_{1}$ be defined in step 1, $\lambda_{k}$ by step 5 for $k\geq 2$ , and $e^{k+1}$ by step 4. $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}=4^{i-1}\lambda_{1}\qquad\forall 1\leq i\leq k,$ (44) $\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\bar{u}^{i}=4\lambda_{i-1}\frac{\bar{u}^{i-1}+3u^{i}}{4}=\lambda_{i-1}\bar{u}^{i-1}+(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i-1})u^{i}\qquad\forall 2\leq i\leq k.$ (45)

$0\in(F+B)(u_{*}^{i})-\lambda_{i}(\bar{u}^{i}-u_{*}^{i})\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lambda_{i}(\bar{u}^{i}-u_{*}^{i})\in(F+B)(u_{*}^{i})\qquad\forall 1\leq i\leq k.$ (46)

Let $u^{*}$ be the unique saddle point of ( 2 ), and let $F$ and $B$ be defined in ( 3 ). $\frac{\mu_{y}}{\lambda_{1}}=\frac{\mu_{y}\|u^{1}-u^{0}\|}{\epsilon}\leq\frac{9\mu_{y}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|}{8\epsilon}.$

$\displaystyle 129(N_{\mathcal{A}}+256\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{A}}+3)\left(9+2\log_{4}\!\left(1+\frac{9\mu_{y}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|}{8\epsilon}\right)\right)\frac{L_{\nabla f}}{\sqrt{\mu_{x}\mu_{y}}}$ $\displaystyle\leq 2^{11}(N_{\mathcal{A}}+256\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{A}}+3)\frac{L_{\nabla f}}{\sqrt{\mu_{x}\mu_{y}}}\left(1+\log_{4}\!\left(1+\frac{\mu_{y}\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|}{\epsilon}\right)\right),$

4.3 Proof of the main result in Subsection 2.3

In this subsection we provide a proof of Theorem 3 . We first establish the residual inclusion and evaluation bound for each trial of Algorithm 3 .

Suppose Assumptions 2 and 3 hold. Let $F$ and $B$ be defined in ( 3 ). We now prove Theorem 3 .

Let $u^{*}$ be the saddle point of ( 2 ). By step 1 of Algorithm 3 , one has

$\displaystyle\mu_{x}\|d\|^{2}\leq\langle\nabla_{x}f(u_{x}^{0}+d,u_{y}^{0})-\nabla_{x}f(u_{x}^{0},u_{y}^{0}),d\rangle\leq\|\nabla_{x}f(u_{x}^{0}+d,u_{y}^{0})-\nabla_{x}f(u_{x}^{0},u_{y}^{0})\|\|d\|=\rho\|d\|^{2},$

where the first inequality follows from the $\mu_{x}$ -strong convexity of $f(\cdot,u_{y}^{0})$ , the second follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, and the equality follows from the definition of $\rho$ . Since $d\neq 0$ and both points belong to $\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}$ , we obtain $\mu_{x}\leq\rho$ . Using this, Assumption 2 , and the Lipschitz continuity of $\nabla f$ , we have $0<\mu_{y}\leq\mu_{x}\leq\rho\leq L_{\nabla f}$ .

By step 2 and the resolvent identity, one has

$\rho(u^{0}-u^{1})-F(u^{0})\in B(u^{1}),\qquad r^{1}=\rho(u^{0}-u^{1})+F(u^{1})-F(u^{0})\in(F+B)(u^{1}).$

$\displaystyle\rho\|u^{0}-u^{1}\|^{2}$ $\displaystyle\leq\langle\rho(u^{0}-u^{*})+F(u^{0})-F(u^{*}),u^{0}-u^{1}\rangle$ $\displaystyle\leq(\rho+L_{\nabla f})\|u^{0}-u^{*}\|\|u^{0}-u^{1}\|,$

$i_{*}=\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{L_{\nabla f}}{\rho}\right\rceil,\qquad j_{*}=\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{\rho}{\mu_{x}}\right\rceil,\qquad k_{*}=\left\lceil\log_{2}\frac{\rho}{\mu_{y}}\right\rceil.$

where the term $3$ accounts for the evaluations in steps 1 and 2, the first inequality follows from $(q+2)^{2}\leq(q_{*}+2)^{2}$ for $q\leq q_{*}$ and $\sum_{q=0}^{q_{*}}2^{-(q_{*}-q)/2}\leq 2+\sqrt{2}$ , and the last inequality follows from ( 57 ), ( 58 ), and the monotonicity of the logarithm and ceiling functions. $r^{t}=A(\hat{x},\hat{y})-A(x^{0},y^{0})+\bigl((2\beta_{t}-\beta_{1})(\hat{x}-x^{0}),(\mu-\beta_{1})(\hat{y}-y^{0})\bigr).$

By ( 66 ), $\|r^{t}\|\leq(L_{\nabla h}+2\beta_{t})\|(\hat{x},\hat{y})-(x^{0},y^{0})\|\leq 5L_{\nabla h}\|(\hat{x},\hat{y})-(x^{0},y^{0})\|$ . Combining these inequalities, we obtain

$\displaystyle\frac{64}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\|r_{x}^{t}\|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\mu}\|r_{y}^{t}\|^{2}}\leq\frac{320L_{\nabla h}}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{t}}{\mu}}\left(2D_{\mathbf{y}}+\frac{4L_{\nabla h}D_{\mathbf{y}}+\sqrt{2L_{\nabla h}\Delta_{0}}}{\beta_{1}}\right).$ (68)

Suppose now that a trial before $t$ has been accepted, and let $i<t$ be the last such trial. Then $(\hat{x},\hat{y})=(x^{i+1},y^{i+1})$ and $x^{t}=x^{i+1}$ . The residual correction in every intervening rejected trial is zero, so step 7 implies $r^{t}=e^{i+1}-\bigl(2\beta_{i}(x^{i+1}-x^{i}),0\bigr)$ . Using this, $\|e^{i+1}\|\leq(\epsilon/64)\sqrt{\mu/\beta_{i}}$ , ( 64 ), and ( 66 ), we obtain

$\displaystyle\frac{64}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\|r_{x}^{t}\|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\mu}\|r_{y}^{t}\|^{2}}$ $\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{t}}{\beta_{i}}}+\frac{128\beta_{i}}{\epsilon}\|x^{i+1}-x^{i}\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2L_{\nabla h}}{\beta_{1}}}+\frac{128}{\epsilon}\sqrt{2L_{\nabla h}\left(\Delta_{0}+\frac{\epsilon D_{\mathbf{y}}}{8}\right)}.$

Using this, ( 66 ), ( 68 ), and $D_{\mathbf{y}}\geq 1$ , we obtain in both cases

$\displaystyle 1+\log_{4}\!\left(1+\frac{64}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\|r_{x}^{t}\|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\mu}\|r_{y}^{t}\|^{2}}\right)$ (69) $\displaystyle\leq 1+\log_{4}\!\left(2^{20}\left(1+\frac{L_{\nabla h}\Delta_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{L_{\nabla h}D_{\mathbf{y}}}{\epsilon}\right)^{5/2}\right)$ $\displaystyle=11+\frac{5}{2}\log_{4}\!\left(1+\frac{L_{\nabla h}\Delta_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{L_{\nabla h}D_{\mathbf{y}}}{\epsilon}\right)\leq 11+\frac{2L_{\nabla h}\Delta_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{2L_{\nabla h}D_{\mathbf{y}}}{\epsilon},$

where the last inequality follows from $\log(1+v)\leq v$ for $v\geq 0$ . Combining ( 67 ) and ( 69 ), we obtain that the numbers of evaluations at trial $t$ are bounded, respectively, by

$2\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{A}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{t}}{\mu}}\left(11+\frac{2L_{\nabla h}\Delta_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{2L_{\nabla h}D_{\mathbf{y}}}{\epsilon}\right).$ (70)

Every later trial $s$ in this group follows an accepted trial $s-1$ with the same parameters. By step 7 and $\|e^{s}\|\leq\tau_{t}$ , one has

$\frac{64}{\epsilon}\sqrt{\|r_{x}^{s}\|^{2}+\frac{\beta_{t}}{\mu}\|r_{y}^{s}\|^{2}}\leq 1+\frac{128\beta_{t}}{\epsilon}\|x^{s}-x^{s-1}\|.$

By ( 63 ), $\beta_{t}\|x^{s}-x^{s-1}\|/\epsilon>1/4$ . Using this, ( 67 ), and $1+\log_{4}(2+128v)\leq 64v^{2}$ for $v\geq 1/4$ , we obtain that the numbers of evaluations at trial $s$ are bounded, respectively, by

$128\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{A}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{t}}{\mu}}\,\frac{\beta_{t}^{2}\|x^{s}-x^{s-1}\|^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}.$ (71)

Each such trial follows a distinct accepted trial. By ( 64 ), ( 66 ), ( 70 ), and ( 71 ), summing the trial bounds yields the following respective bounds for the numbers of evaluations of $h$ , $\nabla h$ , $\prox_{p}$ , and $\prox_{q}$ in this group:

$\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{A}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{t}}{\mu}}\left(22+\frac{260L_{\nabla h}\Delta_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{36L_{\nabla h}D_{\mathbf{y}}}{\epsilon}\right)\leq 2^{9}\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{A}}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{t}}{\mu}}\left(1+\frac{L_{\nabla h}\Delta_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{L_{\nabla h}D_{\mathbf{y}}}{\epsilon}\right).$ (72)

Let $s_{1}<\cdots<s_{J}$ be the first indices of these groups. By step 7, $\beta_{s_{j+1}}=2\beta_{s_{j}}$ for every $1\leq j<J$ . Using this, ( 66 ), and $\mu=\epsilon/(32D)$ , we obtain

$\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{s_{j}}}{\mu}}$ $\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{s_{J}}}{\mu}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}2^{-(J-j)/2}\leq(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{\frac{2L_{\nabla h}}{\mu}}=8(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{\frac{L_{\nabla h}D}{\epsilon}}.$

Summing ( 72 ) and adding the two gradient evaluations and one evaluation of each proximal mapping in step 2, we obtain that the numbers of evaluations of $h$ , $\nabla h$ , $\prox_{p}$ , and $\prox_{q}$ in the run are bounded, respectively, by

$2+2^{12}(2+\sqrt{2})\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}_{\mathcal{A}}\sqrt{\frac{L_{\nabla h}D}{\epsilon}}\left(1+\frac{L_{\nabla h}\Delta_{0}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{L_{\nabla h}D_{\mathbf{y}}}{\epsilon}\right).$ (73)

With $D=2D_{\mathbf{y}}$ , Lemma 12 excludes failure, so Algorithm 4 returns an $\epsilon$ -stationary point of ( 1 ). Since $D_{\mathbf{y}}\geq 1$ and $\epsilon<32L_{\nabla h}$ , ( 73 ) implies ( 19 ). Hence the conclusion follows. ∎

We first prove by induction that every reached phase $k$ satisfies $D_{k}\leq 4D_{\mathbf{y}}$ and its call to Algorithm 4 terminates. Since $D_{1}=1\leq D_{\mathbf{y}}$ , the claim holds for $k=1$ by Lemma 12 . Suppose it holds at a reached phase $k$ . If phase $k+1$ is reached, the call at phase $k$ returned failure. By Lemma 12 , $D_{k}<2D_{\mathbf{y}}$ and hence $D_{k+1}=2D_{k}<4D_{\mathbf{y}}$ . Applying the same lemma at phase $k+1$ proves the induction claim.

Since $D_{k}=2^{k-1}$ and Lemma 12 excludes failure when $D_{k}\geq 2D_{\mathbf{y}}$ , only finitely many phases are reached. Let $K$ be the last phase. By Lemma 12 , its output is an $\epsilon$ -stationary point of ( 1 ). Moreover, $D_{K}\leq 4D_{\mathbf{y}}$ , so

$\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sqrt{D_{k}}=\sqrt{D_{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}2^{-(K-k)/2}\leq(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{D_{K}}\leq(2+\sqrt{2})\sqrt{4D_{\mathbf{y}}}.$

Every phase starts from $(x^{0},y^{0})$ with the same tolerance, so $\Delta_{0}$ is unchanged. Since $D_{k}\geq 1$ and $\epsilon<32L_{\nabla h}$ , summing ( 73 ) with $D=D_{k}$ and using the last inequality yields ( 20 ), including the initialization in every phase. Hence the conclusion follows. ∎
