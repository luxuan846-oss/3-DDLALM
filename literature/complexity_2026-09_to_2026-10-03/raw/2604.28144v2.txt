Global Optimality for Constrained Exploration via Penalty Regularization

Efficient exploration is a central problem in reinforcement learning and is often formalized as maximizing the entropy of the state-action occupancy measure. While unconstrained maximum-entropy exploration is relatively well understood, real-world exploration is often constrained by safety, resource, or imitation requirements. This constrained setting is particularly challenging because entropy maximization lacks additive structure, rendering Bellman-equation-based methods inapplicable. Moreover, scalable approaches require policy parameterization, inducing non-convexity in both the objective and the constraints. To our knowledge, the only prior model-free policy-gradient approach for this setting under general policy parameterization is due to Ying et al. 2025 [ 135 ] . Unfortunately, their guarantees are limited to weak regret and ergodic averages, which do not imply that the final output is a single deployable policy that is near-optimal and nearly feasible. In this work we take a different approach to this problem, and propose Policy Gradient Penalty (PGP) method, a single-loop policy-space method that enforces general convex occupancy-measure constraints via quadratic-penalty regularization. PGP constructs pseudo-rewards that yield gradient estimates of the penalized objective, subsequently exploiting the classical Policy Gradient Theorem. We further establish the regularity of the penalized objective, providing the smoothness properties needed to justify the convergence of PGP. Leveraging hidden convexity and strong duality, we then establish global last-iterate convergence guarantees, attaining an ${\varepsilon}$ -optimal constrained entropy value with ${\varepsilon}$ -bounded constraint violation despite policy-induced non-convexity. We validate PGP through ablations on a grid-world benchmark and further demonstrate scalability on two challenging continuous-control tasks. 1 1 1 Code available under: https://github.com/Flo-Wo/penalty-based-constrained-exploration .

1 Introduction

Efficient exploration is a fundamental challenge in reinforcement learning (RL), particularly in the absence of an external reward signal, where the goal is to acquire broad and informative coverage of the environment rather than optimize task-specific returns. A principled line of work formulates pure exploration as maximizing the entropy of the state-action occupancy measure induced by a policy, encouraging diverse visitation of the environment [ 50 ] . This viewpoint has led to substantial progress in entropy-based exploration, with both theoretical and practical advances [ 90 , 111 ] .

In realistic systems, however, exploration is inherently constrained not only by safety limits and resource budgets, but crucially by imitation requirements, where policies must remain close to a given prior or expert behavior while still exploring novel regions of the state-action space [ 41 ] . Despite their importance, existing approaches to constrained entropy-based exploration are largely heuristic [ 128 , 112 ] . The model-based approaches of Agarwal et al. [2] , Bai et al. [9] operate with explicit representations of the state-action occupancy measure, which limits their applicability in large-scale or continuous settings where function approximation is required. In contrast, Ying et al. [135] provide only ergodic and weak-regret guarantees under restrictive assumptions.

A natural way to model safety, cost, and imitation requirements is to express them as convex constraints on the state-action occupancy measure, yielding a unified and expressive formulation for both finite- and infinite-horizon Markov decision processes. Achieving scalability, however, necessitates policy parameterization, which obscures this convex structure and eliminates the additive Bellman structure underlying standard dynamic-programming and actor-critic methods. Together with enforcing constraints under stochastic, truncated trajectory sampling with biased gradients, this leads to highly challenging optimization problems.

Contributions.

We develop a penalty framework for constrained maximum-entropy exploration that operates directly in policy space while retaining the hidden-convex structure of occupancy-measure optimization. 2 2 2 In Appendix 0.D , we further extend our framework beyond RL to constrained hidden-convex optimization problems with possibly non-smooth objectives and constraints, which could be of independent interest. Our contributions are:

We introduce PGP, a single-loop, primal-only Policy Gradient Penalty method for constrained maximum-entropy exploration that operates directly in policy space and enforces non-convex constraints via a quadratic penalty. By explicitly utilizing the RL structure of the problem, we construct pseudo-rewards , enabling single batch penalty gradient estimation.

We prove global, non-asymptotic last-iterate convergence of PGP under biased stochastic gradients, despite the non-convexity induced by policy parametrization. The analysis quantifies the smoothness of the penalty formulation, a key property needed to establish global convergence, and provides a principled choice of the penalty regularization parameter.

In the finite-MDP setting covered by our theory, we validate PGP on a grid world, demonstrating robustness to penalty tuning and gradient noise and providing practical guidance for choosing the penalty parameter $\beta$ . Beyond our theory, continuous state-action experiments demonstrate scalability and stable late-iterate feasibility.

2 Related Work

Entropy Maximization.

Maximizing the entropy of the state-action occupancy measure was introduced by Hazan et al. [50] as a principled formulation of reward-free exploration. Subsequent work has focused on improving computational efficiency and practical performance, including non-parametric entropy estimation [ 89 , 90 ] , representation learning [ 107 , 132 ] , and successor-based approaches [ 75 , 76 ] . On the theoretical side, Tiapkin et al. [111] established fast convergence rates for maximum-entropy exploration, while Zamboni et al. [138] , Zamboni et al. [139] extended entropy-based exploration to partially observable Markov decision processes (MDPs). These works focus almost exclusively on the unconstrained setting. While they exploit the convex structure of the entropy objective in the occupancy-measure space, they do not address constraints and therefore cannot guarantee feasibility in safety- or resource-constrained environments.

Constrained Entropy-Based Exploration.

Several recent works have studied constrained entropy maximization empirically. Yang and Spaan [128] propose constrained entropy maximization for safe, task-agnostic exploration, and Tiboni et al. [112] apply entropy maximization under performance chance constraints to domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer in robotics. Related ideas appear in transfer and imitation settings [ 16 , 129 , 59 ] , observer gain tuning [ 60 , 80 ] and system identification under performance constraints [ 121 , 149 ] . However, these methods are heuristic in nature and do not provide (global) convergence guarantees, even in tabular settings.

More broadly, constrained and safe RL has been extensively studied under the Constrained Markov decision process (CMDP) formulation [ 4 ] , for instance, using primal-dual methods [ 37 , 42 , 133 , 77 , 119 , 48 , 36 , 10 , 38 , 70 , 84 , 39 ] , primal-only approaches [ 1 , 24 , 27 , 130 , 125 , 116 , 97 , 21 ] , and in Bayesian settings [ 5 , 120 , 6 ] .

Convex Reinforcement Learning.

Unconstrained convex, or general-utility, RL generalizes classical RL by allowing the objective to be a convex functional of the state-action occupancy measure, instead of standard additive rewards [ 137 ] . In the unconstrained setting, Zhang et al. [144] establish global convergence guarantees in tabular MDPs, with subsequent extensions to function approximation and variance reduction [ 146 , 11 , 106 ] , as well as to large-scale problems [ 51 , 12 ] . Finite-trial formulations [ 91 ] have also been studied [ 92 , 93 ] . Related directions include multi-agent convex RL [ 134 ] , a mean-field perspective [ 47 ] , and extensions to risk-sensitive MDPs [ 122 ] , submodular objectives [ 100 , 34 ] , and robust convex RL [ 23 ] . Online variants of unconstrained convex RL have been investigated in [ 86 , 82 , 87 , 13 ] . Closely related in spirit, Kalogiannis et al. [58] study policy gradient convergence in imperfect-information extensive-form games. In the optimization literature, Fatkhullin et al. [44] analyze algorithms for unconstrained stochastic optimization problems under hidden convexity and Fatkhullin et al. [45] develop algorithms for hidden convex constrained problems. We refer to Appendix 0.A for additional related work and a detailed technical comparison of our contributions to the two most closely related works, Zhang et al. [144] and Fatkhullin et al. [44] .

In contrast, significantly fewer works address convex RL under complex (e.g., safety or imitation) constraints. Agarwal et al. [2] study constrained convex MDPs (CCMDPs) using a model-based approach over the occupancy-measure space with count-based approximations and direct policy parametrization, relying on a strong form of Slater’s condition. Bai et al. [9] relax this requirement to the classical Slater condition and propose a Kullback Leibler (KL) regularized primal-dual algorithm, still operating in a model-based occupancy-measure formulation. More recently, [ 135 ] is the first work to study CCMDPs under a general softmax policy parametrization using a policy-gradient approach in policy parameter space. However, the obtained guarantees are limited to weak regret and ergodic averages, i.e., $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}F_{1}(\theta^{(k)})-F^{\star}\leq\varepsilon$ and $\bigl[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}F_{2}(\theta^{(k)})\bigr]_{+}\leq\varepsilon$ , see for example the discussion in [ 88 ] . Such guarantees permit cancellations in constraint violations and, moreover, are not operational in practice: averaging or sampling from iterates in parameter space is generally ill-defined for policy networks. Relatedly, Zhang et al. [142] study convex constrained MDPs in an offline, model-based setting using primal-dual methods under different assumptions. Overall, existing approaches to constrained convex RL suffer from at least one of the following limitations: they rely on model-based or count-based formulations that do not scale beyond small discrete state-action spaces; they require (forms of) Slater’s condition which might fail to hold in practice; and they provide only ergodic guarantees, precluding at-any-time convergence of the last iterate.

More generally, it was observed for constrained MDPs [ 125 , 54 ] that primal-only methods can be preferable in practice, due to fewer hyperparameters, simpler implementations, and faster convergence resulting from the absence of delayed dual updates.

3 Preliminaries

Notation.

For a finite set $A$ , we denote with $\lvert A\rvert$ the cardinality and with $\Delta(A)$ the $(\lvert A\rvert-1)$ -dimensional simplex, i.e. the space of probability distributions over $A$ . If not stated differently, $\lVert\,\cdot\,{}\rVert$ denotes the standard $\ell^{2}$ -norm for vectors and the spectral norm for matrices, and $\langle\,\cdot\,{},\,\cdot\,{}\rangle$ is the Euclidean inner product. A differentiable function $F:{\Theta}\to{\mathbb{R}}$ is $L$ -smooth if the gradient is $L$ -Lipschitz continuous, $L\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}$ . For an integer $k\in{\mathbb{N}}$ define the set $[k]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,k\}$ . A notation ${\OOSym\!\left(\,\cdot\,{}\right)}$ captures all dependencies except for the accuracy ${\varepsilon}$ and ${\OOTildeSym\!\left(\,\cdot\,{}\right)}$ hides logarithmic terms in $\nicefrac{{1}}{{{\varepsilon}}}$ .

Markov Decision Process (MDP).

A discrete-time infinite-horizon discounted Markov Decision Process is a tuple ${\mathbb{M}}\coloneqq({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}},r,{\mathbb{P}},\gamma,\mu_{0})$ , where ${\mathcal{S}}$ and ${\mathcal{A}}$ are finite sets of states and actions, respectively. At a discrete timestep $t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}$ , being in state ${s}_{t}\in{\mathcal{S}}$ taking an action ${a}_{t}\in{\mathcal{A}}$ yields a reward $r_{t}=r({s}_{t},{a}_{t})$ , according to the reward function $r:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to[-r_{\max},r_{\max}]$ , and a new state ${s}_{t+1}\sim{\mathbb{P}}(\,\cdot\,{}|{s}_{t},{a}_{t})$ according to the transition probability kernel ${\mathbb{P}}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to\Delta({\mathcal{S}})$ . $r_{\max}>0$ gives an upper and lower bound on the reward. We define the value function of a trajectory $\tau\coloneqq({s}_{t},{a}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}$ as the cumulative discounted reward $V(\tau)\coloneqq(1-\gamma)\cdot\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r({s}_{t},{a}_{t}),$ where $\mu_{0}$ denotes the initial state distribution, i.e. ${s}_{0}\sim\mu_{0}$ , and $\gamma\in(0,1)$ is the so-called discount factor. A mapping $\pi:{\mathcal{S}}\to\Delta({\mathcal{A}})$ is called a (stationary) policy , and $\Pi$ denotes set of all stationary policies. Combined with the initial distribution $\mu_{0}$ , it induces a probability measure over finite-length trajectories $\tau=({s}_{t},{a}_{t})_{t=0}^{T-1}$ by sampling ${a}_{t}\sim\pi(\,\cdot\,{}|{s}_{t})$ at each timestep $t=0,\ldots,T$ , $T\in{\mathbb{N}}$ , i.e. $p(\tau)\coloneqq\mu_{0}({s}_{0})\pi({a}_{0}|{s}_{0})\cdot\prod_{t=1}^{T-1}{\mathbb{P}}({s}_{t}|{s}_{t-1},{a}_{t-1})\pi({a}_{t}|{s}_{t})$ .

Policy Parametrization.

Throughout this work, we will assume the common softmax policy parameterization

$\displaystyle\pi_{\theta}({a}|{s})\coloneqq\frac{\exp(\psi({s},{a};\theta))}{\sum_{{a}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\exp(\psi({s},{a}^{\prime};\theta))},$

where $\theta\in\Theta\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{p}$ , ${s}\in{\mathcal{S}}$ , ${a}\in{\mathcal{A}}$ , and a smooth function $\psi:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times\Theta\to{\mathbb{R}}$ .

State-action Occupancy Measure (OM).

For any policy $\pi_{\theta}$ , we define the (state-action) occupancy measure $\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to[0,1]$ as

$\displaystyle\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}({s},{a})\coloneqq(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu_{0},\pi_{\theta}}({s}_{t}={s},{a}_{t}={a}),$

where ${\mathbb{P}}_{\mu_{0},\pi_{\theta}}$ denotes the probability distribution of the Markov chain $({s}_{t},{a}_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{N}}}$ induced by the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and the initial state distribution $\mu_{0}$ under the transition probability kernel ${\mathbb{P}}$ . We denote by $\Lambda\coloneqq\{\lambda^{\pi}\;:\;\pi\in\Pi\}$ the set of all state-action occupancy measures induced by the stationary policies $\Pi$ . Since $\lvert{\mathcal{S}}\rvert,\lvert{\mathcal{A}}\rvert<\infty$ , we will interpret a $\lambda\in\Lambda$ as an element of $[0,1]^{\lvert{\mathcal{S}}\rvert\times\lvert{\mathcal{A}}\rvert}$ . For a parametrized policy, we will use the notations $\lambda(\theta)$ and $\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}$ interchangeably. Intuitively, since the OM captures the state-action visitation frequencies induced by a policy, it provides a principled foundation for defining exploration as maximizing the entropy of the induced state-action distribution.

4 Problem Formulation

For a convex, $L$ -smooth constraint ${\mathcal{R}}:\Lambda\to{\mathbb{R}}$ formulated in the state-action-occupancy measure, we formulate the following constrained-max-entropy problem:

$\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in\Theta}F_{1}(\theta)\coloneqq{\mathcal{H}}(\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}})=-{\mathbb{E}}_{({s},{a})\sim\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}}\!\left[\log\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}({s},{a})\right]\;\;\mathrm{s.t.}\;\;F_{2}(\theta)\coloneqq{\mathcal{R}}(\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}})\leq 0.$ (CME)

We assume only that there exists a stationary policy $\pi_{\rm feas}\in\Pi$ satisfying ${\mathcal{R}}(\lambda^{\pi_{\rm feas}})\leq 0$ . Since $\Lambda$ is a compact polytope in a finite MDP, and ${\mathcal{H}}$ and ${\mathcal{R}}$ are continuous, the corresponding problem over all stationary policies admits an optimizer $\pi^{*}\in\Pi$ . We denote its value by ${\mathcal{H}}^{*}\coloneqq{\mathcal{H}}(\lambda^{\pi^{*}})$ and set ${F^{*}}\coloneqq{\mathcal{H}}^{*}$ . We call ${\hat{\theta}}\in\Theta$ an $({\varepsilon},{\varepsilon})$ -optimal solution if $\lvert{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[F_{1}({\hat{\theta}})-{F^{*}}\right]\rvert\leq{\varepsilon}$ and ${\mathbb{E}}\!\left[[F_{2}({\hat{\theta}})]_{+}\right]\leq{\varepsilon}$ , where ${\varepsilon}>0$ . At first sight, optimizing ( CME ) over $\theta\in\Theta$ , rather than directly over all stationary policies, may appear restrictive. The following proposition shows that the (anchored) tabular softmax family is dense in the set of stationary policies. Theorem 0.C.2 (Appendix) then shows that this density is sufficient for the stated approximate objective and feasibility guarantees.

Let ${\mathbb{M}}$ be a finite MDP with an initial distribution $\min_{{s}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\mu_{0}({s})>0$ , and fix a reference action ${a}_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}$ . Consider the anchored tabular logits $\psi({s},{a}_{0};\theta)=0$ and $\psi({s},{a};\theta)=\theta_{{s},{a}}$ for ${a}\neq{a}_{0}$ , with parameter space $\Theta_{R}=[-R,R]^{{\mathcal{S}}\times({\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{{a}_{0}\})}$ . For every stationary policy $\pi^{*}\in\Pi$ , including deterministic ones, and every ${\varepsilon}\in(0,1]$ , there exist $R={\OOSym\!\left(\log(\left|{\mathcal{A}}\right|/{\varepsilon})\right)}$ and a parameter $\theta\in\Theta_{R}$ such that

$\displaystyle\max_{{s}\in{\mathcal{S}}}\;\lVert\pi_{\theta}(\,\cdot\,{}|{s})-\pi^{*}(\,\cdot\,{}|{s})\rVert_{1}\leq{\varepsilon},$

i.e. the family of anchored tabular softmax classes $\bigcup_{R>0}\{\pi_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta_{R}\}$ is dense in the set of stationary policies.

Full details in Appendix 0.C , we show that fixing the reference logit makes the parameter-to-occupancy map invertible with a Lipschitz inverse on $\Theta_{R}$ . ∎

The problem formulation ( CME ) includes, but is not limited to, the following examples :

(Cumulative Cost) A safety constraint can be given by the discounted cumulative cost $F_{2}(\theta)\coloneqq(1-\gamma){\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{0},\pi_{\theta}}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\,c(s_{t},a_{t})\right]-{c_{\max}}$ , for a cost vector $c\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}$ . In this case ( CME ) corresponds to finding the policy with the maximum entropy among all the policies solving the environment ${\mathcal{R}}(\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}})\coloneqq\langle c,\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}\rangle-{c_{\max}}$ .

(Apprenticeship Learning) Closeness to an expert’s or prior trajectory can be encoded using the KL divergence ${\mathcal{R}}(\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}})\coloneqq{\klDiv\left(\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}\lVert\lambda^{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}\right)}-{\mathcal{R}_{\max}}$ , with respect to the state-action-occupancy measure of a reference policy $\lambda^{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}$ .

Naturally, our framework can be extended to multiple constraints, by considering a vector-valued ${\mathcal{R}}$ and dealing with the Jacobian instead of the gradient.

Pseudo-rewards and single-trajectory-batch penalty gradients. A key algorithmic novelty lies in the construction of pseudo-rewards $g^{(k)}_{\diamond}$ , $\diamond\in\{O,C\}$ , introduced in Line 5 . In generic stochastic optimization, both, exact and quadratic penalty formulations require applying the (generalized) chain rule through the constraint function, which canonically necessitates separate stochastic estimators for the function value and its (sub-)gradient. This mismatch induces bias and has led prior work on stochastic penalty methods and ALM to either restrict attention to deterministic constraints [ 62 , 117 , 118 ] , incur additional sample complexity via multiple gradient or function evaluations per iteration [ 26 , 78 , 127 ] or rely on momentum and variance-tracking schemes to control the resulting bias [ 73 , 68 ] .

Our method fundamentally departs from this paradigm by explicitly exploiting the RL structure of the problem. Leveraging the Policy Gradient Theorem ( PG-Thm ), we reinterpret gradients of both the objective and the penalty term as policy gradients with respect to constructed pseudo-rewards. This yields the estimator in ( ∇ θ -Est ), which allows us to compute a stochastic gradient of the penalized objective using a single trajectory batch, without auxiliary function-value estimation.

Importantly, the occupancy measure is estimated only once from the full batch, and the resulting common pseudo-reward is reused on every trajectory in that same batch. In particular, the quadratic-penalty gradient contains $2\beta[{\mathcal{R}}(\lambda)]_{+}\nabla{\mathcal{R}}(\lambda)$ , whose two factors both depend on the unknown occupancy measure. Using the same trajectory batch to estimate these factors and form the policy-gradient estimate therefore yields a dependent and biased estimator. We show in Lemmas 0.B.2 and 0.B.5 that our pseudo-reward construction controls both truncation and occupancy-estimation bias, as well as the estimator variance, without requiring a model, an exact value function, nested sampling, or auxiliary function evaluations.

5.1 Theoretical Analysis

The key theoretical insight is the hidden convex structure induced by the occupancy-measure formulation. By mapping a policy $\pi$ to its corresponding occupancy measure, the penalized problem becomes convex and admits a one-step contraction inequality under biased stochastic gradients. Translating this contraction back to policy space allows us to unroll the recursion over iterations, yielding global last-iterate convergence guarantees. We make the following (standard) assumptions:

The function $\psi:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times\Theta\to{\mathbb{R}}$ satisfies $\psi\in C^{2}({\mathcal{S}},{\mathcal{A}},\Theta;{\mathbb{R}})$ , and there exist constants ${\ell_{\psi}},{L_{\psi}}>0$ such that (i) $\max_{{s}\in{\mathcal{S}},{a}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\psi({s},{a},\theta)\rVert\leq{\ell_{\psi}}$ and (ii) $\max_{{s}\in{\mathcal{S}},{a}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\lVert\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\psi({s},{a},\theta)\rVert\leq{L_{\psi}}$ .

Under these assumptions, we show the regularity of the score function $\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(\,\cdot\,{})$ in Appendix 0.B .

The parameter-to-occupancy mapping $\lambda:\Theta\to\Lambda$ , $\theta\mapsto\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}$ , satisfies:

(Locally Invertible) For every parameter $\theta\in\Theta$ there exists a neighborhood $\theta\in\mathcal{U}_{\theta}\subset\Theta$ such that $\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}|_{\mathcal{U}_{\theta}}$ is a bijection between $\mathcal{U}_{\theta}$ and ${\mathcal{V}}_{\theta}\coloneqq\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}(\mathcal{U}_{\theta})$ . A (local) inverse is $\lambda^{-1}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\theta}}:{\mathcal{V}}_{\theta}\to\mathcal{U}_{\theta}$ .

(Lipschitz Inverse) The inverse $\lambda^{-1}_{{\mathcal{V}}_{\theta}}$ is ${\mu_{\lambda}}$ -Lipschitz continuous over $\Theta.$

(Locally Hidden Convex) There exists an ${\varepsilon}_{\lambda}>0$ such that for all ${\varepsilon}\in(0,{\varepsilon}_{\lambda}]$ and all $\theta\in\Theta$ the convex combination satisfies $(1-{\varepsilon})\lambda^{\pi_{\theta}}+{\varepsilon}\lambda^{\pi^{\star}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{\theta}$ .

The above assumption is common and was verified in a tabular setting, e.g., [ 144 , Prop. H.1] . For the (anchored) tabular softmax parametrization above, we verify Assumption 5.1 and Assumption 5.2 directly in Appendix 0.C . Note that since $\Lambda$ is a polytope ( $\Lambda\subset[0,1]^{{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}}$ ), its domain is bounded, i.e., there exists ${\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}}>0$ such that $\lVert\lambda(\theta)-\lambda(\theta^{\prime})\rVert\leq{\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}},$ for any $\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta$ . Due to the (local) Lipschitzness of the inverse we can also uniformly bound the domain $\Theta$ by a diameter ${\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}$ , cf. Thm. 3.2 in Zhang et al. [144]

However, the sample complexity $N\cdot B$ crucially depends on the parameter $\beta$ , coming from the estimate of the smoothness constant $L_{P,\theta}.$ In what follows, we will translate the result to convergence to an $({\varepsilon},{\varepsilon})$ -optimal solution and give a recommendation on the parameter $\beta$ trading the feasibility and sample complexity. We use the following standard assumption.

We assume strong duality holds for ( CME ), i.e. for ${\theta}^{*}$ there exists a Lagrangian multiplier ${\nu^{*}}\geq 0$ such that ${d^{*}}=d({\nu^{*}})=-{F^{*}}$ , where the (Lagrangian) dual function is defined as the infimum of the Lagrangian with respect to the primal variable ${\theta}$ , that is, $d(\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{{\theta}\in{\Theta}}L({\theta},\nu)=\inf_{{\theta}\in{\Theta}}\bigl(-F_{1}({\theta})+\nu F_{2}({\theta})\bigr)$ , and the corresponding dual problem as ${d^{*}}\coloneqq\sup_{\nu\geq 0}d(\nu)=\sup_{\nu\geq 0}\inf_{{\theta}\in{\Theta}}L({\theta},\nu)$ .

It is well known that, in general for any $\nu\geq 0$ , we have $d(\nu)\leq-{F^{*}}$ , i.e. weak duality always holds [ 96 ] . Assumption 5.5 is not required for convergence to the penalty objective in Theorem 5.4 ; it is used only for the subsequent translation to objective and feasibility guarantees through a finite multiplier. Most importantly, it is strictly weaker than Slater’s condition: Slater is a sufficient certificate for strong duality [ 96 ] , but is not necessary. Consequently, strong duality permits boundary-only feasible sets and equality constraints for which Slater fails [ 45 ] . Under linear safety or performance constraints, a feasible point is already sufficient, cf. Bertsekas [15, Prop. 5.3.1] .

7 Concluding Remarks & Future Work

We propose a single-loop, policy-space penalty method for constrained maximum-entropy exploration. Our analysis provides global last-iterate convergence guarantees under strong duality, despite non-convex policy parameterization and stochastic gradients, and we demonstrate the scalability of our method on a continuous control task. Future work includes extensions to non-smooth occupancy-measure constraints. On the algorithmic side, closing the theory-practice gap in step-size and batch-size selection remains an important and non-trivial challenge. Finally, our current algorithm and analysis yield a relatively large $\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}({\varepsilon}^{-6})$ sample complexity. We believe this sample complexity can be reduced by using additional algorithmic refinements, such as variance reduction or adaptive penalty schemes. However, a rigorous validation of these directions remains open.

Appendix 0.A Additional Related Work

We provide additional related work and make precise why our contributions are substantively different from the two most closely related works.

Different Entropy Formulations.

Other works deal with Rényi entropy formulation [ 141 , 136 ] , geometric aware Shannon entropy [ 49 ] , and f-Divergence entropy regularization [ 3 ] . Lee et al. [67] , Islam et al. [53] recast exploration in terms of marginal state distribution learning, while Jain et al. [55] focus on entropy maximization of a single trajectory, Zamboni et al. [140] on a multi-agent RL setting, and Zisselman et al. [148] use ensemble agents to ensure exploration at test-time.

Optimization Literature.

Our algorithmic framework is most closely related to penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods (ALM) for constrained optimization. Such methods are classical tools with a long history and broad adoption [ 25 , 46 , 14 , 20 , 96 ] , and have been extensively studied from a first-order complexity perspective in both convex and non-convex settings [ 8 , 64 , 94 , 71 ] . However, existing analyses typically focus on deterministic optimization, establish asymptotic convergence or convergence to ${\varepsilon}$ -KKT points, and do not directly apply to entropy-based reinforcement learning, where non-convexity arises from policy parameterization and gradients are estimated stochastically.

Recent work has also highlighted naively incorporating constraints as costs in the objective function may fail to simultaneously ensure feasibility and optimality in deep learning settings [ 102 ] . Our results demonstrate that, when combined with problem-specific gradient structure, quadratic penalty methods can yield strong guarantees even in stochastic, non-convex RL problems. A unified view connecting our approach to proximal-point and ALM frameworks under hidden convexity is developed in Appendix 0.D .

0.A.1 Detailed Comparison to Zhang et al. [144]

Problem.

Zhang et al. [144] study unconstrained general-utility RL, i.e., maximizing a convex functional $F(\lambda^{\pi})$ without constraints; introducing constraints changes the optimization landscape fundamentally. Moreover, Example 2.1 therein is restricted to cumulative discounted reward in a standard constrained MDP via a generic penalty function, leaving both the concrete choice of penalty and the specific objective unspecified.

Algorithm.

The core algorithmic novelty is not in running biased SGD per se, but in how we construct the gradient estimator for the penalized objective. In generic stochastic penalty and ALM methods, the chain rule through $\beta\,{[{\mathcal{R}}(\lambda(\theta))]_{+}^{2}}$ canonically requires separate stochastic estimators for the constraint function value ${\mathcal{R}}(\lambda(\theta))$ and its sub-gradient; their product creates a cross-term bias that does not vanish with batch size. This fundamental bias mismatch has forced the prior stochastic penalty and ALM methods to either (i) restrict to deterministic constraints, (ii) use nested sampling or multiple gradient evaluations per iteration, or (iii) rely on momentum or variance-tracking. Our pseudo-reward construction (Line 5 of Section 5 ) circumvents this entirely by reinterpreting both gradient terms as policy gradients with respect to constructed pseudo-rewards via the Policy Gradient Theorem. This construction is specific to the RL structure and does not appear in, and cannot be derived from, Zhang et al. [144] , which operates in the unconstrained setting.

Theory.

Zhang et al. [144] explicitly assume exact knowledge of the Q-function (cf. the Remark after their Theorem 2); our analysis operates in the realistic stochastic setting, requiring a joint control of truncation bias $\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{H})$ and Monte-Carlo variance $\mathcal{O}(B^{-1/2})$ (cf. Lemma 0.B.2 ). Beyond this stochastic extension, two further nontrivial analytical steps are required:

Preservation of hidden convexity under the penalty ( Lemma 0.B.3 ): Showing that composing $\mathcal{R}(\lambda)$ with the quadratic penalty $[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}^{2}$ preserves the smoothness and hidden convexity structure, and deriving the $\beta$ -dependent smoothness constants $(\ell_{P,\lambda},L_{P,\lambda},L_{P,\lambda,\infty})$ , requires careful analysis of the interaction between the penalty nonlinearity and the occupancy-measure geometry. We are not aware of any prior work establishing such a result.

Translation under strong duality ( Corollary 5.6 , Corollary 0.D.22 ): Converting the penalty gap $P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{2,\beta}({\theta^{(N)}})\leq{\varepsilon}$ into simultaneous objective and constraint guarantees requires a quantitative relationship between $\beta(\varepsilon)$ and the dual multiplier $\eta^{*}$ . This translation, yielding the specific scaling $\beta=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})$ , is new to hidden convex optimization.

0.A.2 Detailed Comparison to Fatkhullin et al. [44]

Fatkhullin et al. [44] analyze unconstrained stochastic optimization under hidden convexity with an unbiased gradient oracle. Thus constraints, stochastic biased gradients, and their interaction with hidden convexity are not addressed therein. We borrow the hidden-convexity framework but face two difficulties absent in Fatkhullin et al. [44] .

First, our gradient estimator introduces bias through two distinct mechanisms: (i) trajectory truncation at horizon $H$ , and (ii) the occupancy-measure estimation error propagated through the penalty gradient via the pseudo-rewards, which are evaluated at the estimated occupancy measure ${\widehat{\lambda}}$ rather than the true $\lambda_{H}$ .

Second, the penalty introduces a composite structure whose chain rule canonically induces two independently estimated quantities which we resolve by introducing our pseudo-reward construction.

Controlling the chain of penalty smoothness preservation, through occupancy-measure error propagation, to the final bias-variance tradeoff, is the technical core of our analysis beyond a direct adaptation of Fatkhullin et al. [44] and requires carefully balancing the horizon $H$ , batch size $B$ , as well as the the step-size $\eta$ is a three-way trade-off absent from the unbiased oracle model of Fatkhullin et al. [44] .

Furthermore, translating the penalty guarantees to the constrained problem requires the scaling $\beta={\OOSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-1}\right)}$ from strong duality ( Assumption 5.5 ), a condition entirely absent in the unconstrained framework. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work handles the combination of hidden convex constraints and stochastic biased gradients.

Appendix 0.D Unified Penalty Framework for Deterministic Smooth and Non-Smooth Constrained Optimization under Hidden Convexity

This section places the proposed Policy Gradient Penalty (PGP) method into a broader optimization perspective. We show that the constrained maximum-entropy exploration problem studied in the main paper is a special instance of a wider class of constrained optimization problems exhibiting hidden convexity , and that our analysis extends naturally to both smooth and non-smooth settings.

In particular, we connect our approach to penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods (ALM), proximal-point methods, and recent developments in (non-)convex optimization under hidden convexity. While these connections are not required to understand or apply the RL algorithm proposed in the main paper, they clarify the generality of our framework and highlight how problem-specific structure enables guarantees that are unavailable to generic penalty or ALM schemes.

Proximal-point Methods and Penalty Methods.

Proximal-point methods (PPM), originally introduced by Moreau [85] and Martinet [83] , were later systematically developed and popularized by Rockafellar [103] , Rockafellar [104] , Rockafellar and Wets [105] , with Boyd and Parikh [19] providing a recent overview. In the context of non-smooth and non-convex optimization, PPM appears either implicitly in convergence analyses or explicitly as an algorithmic design principle for unconstrained problems [ 31 , 30 , 29 , 32 , 33 , 44 ] , and more recently in primal-only methods for solving non-smooth, non-convex (deterministic) constrained optimization problems [ 22 , 81 , 52 , 56 , 45 ] . Related ideas have also been explored in multi-agent and game-theoretic settings, e.g., [ 57 ] for constrained Markov games.

Penalty and augmented Lagrangian methods are classical tools for constrained optimization, dating back to early variational approaches [ 25 , 46 ] , and have since been widely adopted and studied [ 14 , 131 , 20 , 96 , 123 ] . A substantial body of work analyzes first-order penalty and ALM schemes with explicit complexity guarantees in convex optimization [ 8 , 64 , 99 , 65 , 94 , 79 , 126 , 147 ] , as well as more recently in non-convex settings [ 71 , 72 ] .

ALM-PPM Hybrids and Hidden Convexity.

The combination of augmented Lagrangian schemes with proximal-point ideas, originally introduced by Auslender et al. [7] , has proven particularly effective. Recent work establishes strong complexity guarantees for smooth non-convex optimization [ 61 , 143 , 124 , 74 ] , as well as for non-smooth but convex problems [ 35 , 114 ] .

As already mentioned in the main part of this work, ( CME ) is a special instance of a broader class of (deterministic) optimization problems, called hidden convex problems, of the form

$\displaystyle\min_{{\theta}\in{\Theta}}\;F_{1}({\theta})\coloneqq H_{1}(c({\theta})),\quad\text{s.t. }F_{2}({\theta})\coloneqq H_{2}(c({\theta}))\leq 0,$ (HC)

where we use the function $c(\,\cdot\,{})$ to indicate a more general transformation, beyond the state-action-occupancy measure we studied in the main part of this work. The following central assumption on $c(\,\cdot\,{})$ is a stronger version of Assumption 5.2 , but the analysis can also be carried out under Assumption 5.2 , cf. Fatkhullin et al. [45] after Definition 1.

The above problem ( HC ) is called hidden convex with modulus $\mu_{c}>0$ , if its components satisfy the following underlying conditions.

The domain ${\Lambda}=c({\Theta})$ is convex, the functions $H_{1},H_{2}:{\Lambda}\to{\mathbb{R}}$ are convex, i.e. satisfy for $i=1,2$ and for all $u,v\in{\Lambda}$ and any $\alpha\in[0,1]$

$\displaystyle H_{i}((1-\alpha){u}+\alpha{v})\leq(1-\alpha)H_{i}({u})+\alpha H_{i}({v})-\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha\mu_{H}}{2}\lVert{u}-{v}\rVert^{2}.$ (HC-1)

Additionally, we assume ( HC ) admits a solution ${u}^{*}\in{\Lambda}$ with its corresponding objective function value ${F^{*}}\coloneqq H_{1}({u}^{*})=F_{1}(c^{-1}({u}^{*}))$ . Throughout this appendix, ${F^{*}}$ denotes the minimum which corresponds to $-{F^{*}}$ from ( CME ).

The map $c:{\Theta}\to{\Lambda}$ is invertible and there exists a $\mu_{c}>0$ such that for all ${\theta},{\tilde{\theta}}\in{\Theta}$ it holds

$\displaystyle\lVert c({\theta})-c({\tilde{\theta}})\rVert\geq\mu_{c}\lVert{\theta}-{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert.$ (HC-2)

If $\mu_{H}>0$ , we refer to ( HC ) as $(\mu_{c},\mu_{H})$ -strongly convex. Note that in this more generic setting, we have $\mu_{c}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{\mu_{\lambda}}}$ of Assumption 5.2 . We can map ( CME ) to ( HC ) via $H_{1}=-{\mathcal{H}}$ and $H_{2}={\mathcal{R}}$ .

Note that the condition ( HC-2 ) along with Assumption 0.D.4 imply that the domain ${\Theta}$ has bounded diameter ${\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{c}}{\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}}$ . For necessary and sufficient conditions for ( HC-2 ), we refer the reader to Fatkhullin et al. [44] .

We start this section with two key inequalities for the analysis of gradient-based methods under hidden convexity, then we analyze biased SGD under hidden convexity and afterwards we generalize our penalty method efforts of the main part to non-smooth problems.

0.D.1 Key Inequalities for Analysis under Hidden Convexity

We state the following proposition which is key for deriving global convergence guarantees under hidden convexity.

Let $F_{i}(\,\cdot\,{})$ , $i=1,2$ , be hidden convex with $\mu_{c}>0$ . For any $\alpha\in[0,1]$ and ${\theta},{\tilde{\theta}}\in{\Theta}$ , define ${\theta}_{\alpha}\coloneqq c^{-1}((1-\alpha)c({\theta})+\alpha c({\tilde{\theta}}))$ , then, for $i=1,2$ , the following functional inequality

$\displaystyle F_{i}({\theta}_{\alpha})\leq(1-\alpha)F_{i}({\theta})+\alpha F_{i}({\tilde{\theta}})-\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha\mu_{H}}{2}\lVert c({\theta})-c({\tilde{\theta}})\rVert^{2},$ (HC-FI)

and norm inequality

$\displaystyle\lVert{\theta}_{\alpha}-{\theta}\rVert\leq\frac{\alpha}{\mu_{c}}\lVert c({\theta})-c({\tilde{\theta}})\rVert.$ (HC-NI)

hold.

The proof is originally presented in Fatkhullin et al. [44] but we recall it here for educational purposes, since it illustrates the core idea of convergence analyses under hidden convexity.

By (strong) convexity of $H_{i}(\,\cdot\,{})$ and the convexity of the domain ${\Lambda}$ , we have

$\displaystyle F_{i}({\theta}_{\alpha})$ $\displaystyle=F_{i}(c^{-1}((1-\alpha)c({\theta})+\alpha c({\tilde{\theta}})))$ $\displaystyle=H_{i}((1-\alpha)c({\theta})+\alpha c({\tilde{\theta}}))$ $\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)H_{i}(c({\theta}))+\alpha H_{i}(c({\tilde{\theta}}))-\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha\mu_{H}}{2}\lVert c({\theta})-c({\tilde{\theta}})\rVert^{2}$ $\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)F_{i}({\theta})+\alpha F_{i}({\tilde{\theta}})-\frac{(1-\alpha)\alpha\mu_{H}}{2}\lVert c({\theta})-c({\tilde{\theta}})\rVert^{2},$

using that $(1-\alpha)c({\theta})+\alpha c({\tilde{\theta}})\in{\Lambda}$ for any ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ , with $i=1,2$ .

Using the property of $c$ we obtain

$\displaystyle\lVert{\theta}_{\alpha}-{\theta}\rVert=\lVert c^{-1}((1-\alpha)c({\theta})+\alpha c({\tilde{\theta}}))-c^{-1}(c({\theta}))\rVert\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{c}}\lVert\alpha(c({\theta})-c({\tilde{\theta}}))\rVert,$

which concludes the proof. ∎

0.D.2 Biased Stochastic Gradient Descent under Hidden Convexity

To solve our penalty problem ( OptGap ), we use (projected) Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with a biased gradient estimate. We analyze biased SGD in our more general, hidden convex setting.

Consider the following general update of projected stochastic gradient descent:

$\displaystyle{\theta}^{(k+1)}\coloneqq\proj_{{\Theta}}\left({\theta}^{(k)}-\eta g^{(k)}\right),\quad g^{(k+1)}\coloneqq\hat{g}^{(k+1)}({\theta}^{(k)},\xi^{(k)}),$ (SGD)

where the stochastic gradient oracle satisfies

$\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\!\left[\hat{g}^{(k)}({\theta},\xi)\right]$ $\displaystyle=\nabla_{{\theta}}F({\theta})+b({\theta})$ (SGD-Bias) $\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\!\left[\left\lVert\hat{g}^{(k)}({\theta},\xi)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\xi}\!\left[\hat{g}^{(k)}({\theta},\xi)\right]\right\rVert^{2}\right]$ $\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{2}$ (SGD-Var)

with variance $\sigma^{2}\geq 0$ and a uniformly bounded bias $\left\lVert b({\theta})\right\rVert^{2}\leq\delta^{2}$ , $\delta\geq 0$ , for all ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ .

Let $F$ be $L$ -smooth and hidden convex with ${F^{*}}\coloneqq F({\theta}^{*})$ for an ${\theta}^{*}\in{\Theta}$ , i.e. $F_{2}\equiv 0$ . Then running (projected) biased ( SGD ), with a step size $\eta\leq\frac{1}{L}$ , yields for every $K\in{\mathbb{N}}$ and any $\alpha\in[0,1]$ the following inequality

$\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[F({\theta}^{(N+1)})-{F^{*}}\right]\leq(1-\alpha)^{N}\Delta_{0}+\frac{{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{{\mu^{2}_{c}}\eta}\alpha+\frac{\eta}{2}\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2}\right)$

with $\Delta_{0}\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[F({\theta}^{(0)})-{F^{*}}\right]$ . In particular, for an arbitrary target accuracy of ${\varepsilon}>0$ , without loss of generality ${\varepsilon}\leq\frac{3{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}L}{{\mu^{2}_{c}}}$ , by picking

$\displaystyle\alpha\leq\frac{{\mu^{2}_{c}}\eta}{{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}\frac{{\varepsilon}}{3}\leq\frac{{\mu^{2}_{c}}}{{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}L}\frac{{\varepsilon}}{3}$

after

$\displaystyle N\geq\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\left(\frac{3\Delta_{0}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\geq\frac{3{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}\cdot L}{{\mu^{2}_{c}}}\frac{1}{{\varepsilon}}\log\left(\frac{3\Delta_{0}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)$

steps, the last iterate satisfies

$\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[F({\theta}^{(N+1)})-{F^{*}}\right]\leq\frac{2{\varepsilon}}{3}+\frac{\eta}{2}\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2}\right).$

Consequently, we need the bias and the variance to satisfy

$\displaystyle\sigma^{2},\delta^{2}\leq\frac{{\varepsilon}}{3}\frac{\alpha}{\eta}\leq\frac{{\mu^{2}_{c}}}{9{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{\varepsilon}^{2}$

in order to get ${\varepsilon}$ -close to the global minima.

We use Thm. 7 in Fatkhullin et al. [44] and that the gradient estimation error can be uniformly bounded by

$\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\left\lVert\hat{g}^{(k)}-\nabla F({\theta})\right\rVert^{2}\right]={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\left\lVert\hat{g}^{(k)}-{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\hat{g}^{(k)}\right]\right\rVert^{2}\right]+{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\left\lVert{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\hat{g}^{(k)}\right]-\nabla F({\theta})\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2}+\delta^{2}.$

Plugging this bound into the proof of Thm. 7 in Fatkhullin et al. [44] , unrolling the recursion and choosing $\alpha$ as proposed concludes the proof. ∎

Inspired by the success of the penalty method for solving ( CME ), the question arises if penalty methods can be used to solve the broader class of constrained hidden convex optimization problems.

We conduct our analysis under the following (standard) assumptions:

(Weak Convexity) $F_{1},F_{2}$ to be $\rho$ -weakly convex, for an $\rho>0$ . It is known that weak convexity is a relatively mild assumption, compared to e.g. smoothness [ 29 , 40 ] . $F_{i}$ is weakly convex, if e.g. $F_{i}$ is twice continuously differentiable with the Hessian satisfying $\nabla^{2}F_{i}({\theta})\succcurlyeq-\rho$ , for all ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ , $i=1,2$ , cf. Prop 4.12 in Vial [115] .

(Bounded [Sub-]Gradients) The functions $F_{i}$ , $i=1,2$ , are continuous and satisfy for all ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ that $\partial F_{i}({\theta})\neq\emptyset$ and with $\lVert g\rVert\leq G$ we have a uniform bound for all sub-gradients $g\in\partial F_{i}({\theta})$ , ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ .

(Bounded Domain) The Domain ${\Lambda}$ has a bounded diameter ${\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}}>0$ .

In particular in the context of non-convex, non-smooth optimization, the proximal point method (PPM) framework [ 19 ] has proven to be very successful in the unconstrained setting [ 31 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 32 , 44 ] as well as in the constrained setting [ 81 , 17 , 52 , 18 , 56 , 45 ] , either implicitly in the analysis or explicitly for algorithm design. Motivated by the non-smooth literature, as well as recent advances in proximal point method penalty [ 74 ] and augmented Lagrangian methods [ 114 , 124 ] , we propose the following, inexact proximal point penalty method :

$\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &{\theta}^{(k)}\approx\hat{{\theta}}^{(k)}\coloneqq\argmin_{{\theta}\in{\Theta}}{{\varphi}^{(k)}}({\theta})\coloneqq F_{1}({\theta})+\frac{{\hat{\rho}}}{2}\lVert{\theta}-{\theta}^{(k)}\rVert^{2}+\beta{{\mathcal{L}}(F_{2}({\theta}))},\end{aligned}$ (IPPPM)

where the penalty function ${\mathcal{L}}$ depends on the smoothness of the constraint. In the case of non-smooth $F_{1},F_{2}$ , the exact penalty function $[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}$ is a natural choice. Under the additional assumption of $L$ -smooth $F_{1},F_{2}$ one needs to “smoothen” the penalty, in order to propagate the smoothness and thus a quadratic penalty ${[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}^{2}}$ is applied. For the convergence analysis of IPPM, we use the notation ${{\mathcal{L}}(\,\cdot\,{})}$ for both penalty functions and perform a unified analysis by using that both penalty functions are convex and monotone, before we compute the corresponding sample complexities for $[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}$ and ${[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}^{2}}$ individually.

Under Assumption 0.D.4 , if ${\hat{\rho}}>\rho$ , the inner penalty problem is strongly convex, see Prop. 2.1 in Davis and Grimmer [30] .

The roadmap of the PPPM analysis is the following:

Preservation of Weak Convexity and Bounded (Sub-)Gradients. Under Assumption 0.D.4 , we show in Section 0.D.5 that the penalty term ${\mathcal{L}}\circ F_{2}$ has uniformly bounded (sub-)gradients and is weakly convex. Thus, ( IPPPM ) is $\rho_{\varphi}$ -weakly convex and can be lifted to be strongly convex. The constant $\rho_{\varphi}$ depends on the penalty function and the smoothness assumption.

Solve inner problem efficiently. We apply an efficient algorithm for the inner (non-)smooth strongly convex unconstrained optimization problems to obtain convergence guarantees for the optimality gap of the penalty function $P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{{\mathcal{L}},\beta}$ .

Translate guarantees. Under the assumption of strong duality , we translate the results into guarantees on the optimality gap and constraint violation. An overview of all of the resulting rates can be found in Table 3 .

We assume to have access to an inner approximate solution oracle ${\mathcal{A}}_{{{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}}$ to solve ( IPPPM ) to an ${{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}$ -accuracy in expectation for any ${{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}>0$ , i.e.

$\displaystyle{{\varphi}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pen}}}({\theta}^{(k)})-{{\varphi}^{(k)}_{\mathrm{pen}}}(\hat{{\theta}}^{(k)})\leq{{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}$

for all iterations $k\in[N]$ after ${T_{\mathrm{in}}}={T_{\mathrm{in}}}({{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}})$ steps.

Exemplarily, for the non-smooth case, we will formulate Corollary 0.D.11 and Corollary 0.D.13 in terms of the classical (stochastic) sub-gradient descent and in the smooth case Corollary 0.D.12 using SGD, respectively.

to achieve the desired inner accuracy, where $\Delta_{0}\coloneqq F_{1}({\theta}^{(0)})-{F^{*}}$ , ${\mathcal{V}_{0}}\coloneqq[F_{2}({\theta}^{(0)})]_{+}$ , $\beta({\nu^{*}})\coloneqq 2{\nu^{*}}+2$ , $\rho_{\varphi}(\beta)\coloneqq\beta({\nu^{*}})\cdot\rho$ , and $G_{\varphi}(\beta)\coloneqq G+\beta({\nu^{*}})\rho{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}+\beta({\nu^{*}})G$ .

$\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{tot}}^{\mathrm{{\mathrm{n}\text{-}sm}}}=N\cdot T_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{SM}}({{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}})={\OOTildeSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-3}\right)}.$

We invoke Lemma 0.D.19 and obtain that each problem ( IPPPM ) is $\rho_{\varphi}$ -weakly convex, with $\rho_{\varphi}=\beta\rho$ and has uniformly bounded gradients by $G_{\varphi}\coloneqq G+\rho_{\varphi}{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}+\beta G$ . Thus, we can lift with ${\hat{\rho}}(\beta)\coloneqq 2\rho_{\varphi}=\beta({\nu^{*}})\rho$ and obtain $(\beta({\nu^{*}})\cdot\rho)$ -strongly convex inner problems. Plugging in the constants into Theorem 0.D.8 yields

$\displaystyle N$ $\displaystyle\geq\frac{3{\hat{\rho}}(\beta)\cdot{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{2\mu_{c}^{2}}\frac{\beta({\nu^{*}})}{{\varepsilon}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{3\beta({\nu^{*}})[\Delta_{0}+\beta({\nu^{*}}){\mathcal{V}_{0}}]}{{\varepsilon}}\right)$ $\displaystyle=\frac{3\beta({\nu^{*}})\rho{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{\mu_{c}^{2}}\frac{\beta({\nu^{*}})}{{\varepsilon}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{3\beta({\nu^{*}})[\Delta_{0}+\beta({\nu^{*}}){\mathcal{V}_{0}}]}{{\varepsilon}}\right)$

for the number of outer iterations. For the inner solver, we use the sub-gradient method (SM) for simplicity with a desired accuracy of

$\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{SM}}({{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}})$ $\displaystyle\geq\frac{4G_{\varphi}(\beta)^{2}}{\beta({\nu^{*}})\rho\cdot{{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}}=\frac{4G_{\varphi}(\beta)^{2}}{\beta({\nu^{*}})\rho}\cdot\frac{3\beta({\nu^{*}})^{2}\rho{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{\mu_{c}^{2}}{\varepsilon}^{-2}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{12\beta({\nu^{*}})G_{\varphi}(\beta)^{2}\cdot{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{\mu_{c}^{2}}{\varepsilon}^{-2}.$

Multiplying both rates concludes the claim. ∎

Under the assumptions of Theorem 0.D.6 , and assuming that $F_{1},F_{2}$ are $L$ -smooth, running Algorithm 2 with the quadratic penalty ${{\mathcal{L}}(\,\cdot\,)}\coloneqq{[\,\cdot\,]_{+}^{2}}$ , ${\hat{\rho}}({\varepsilon})\coloneqq 2\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})$ , and $\beta=\beta({\varepsilon})\coloneqq\frac{2({\nu^{*}}+1)^{2}}{{\varepsilon}}$ yields the guarantee

The total number of gradient evaluations when using the gradient descent (GD) method as the inner solver, is given by:

$\displaystyle\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})=\beta({\varepsilon})\cdot G{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}\cdot\min\{\rho,L\}={\OOSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-1}\right)},$

and $L_{\varphi}$ -smooth, where

$\displaystyle L_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})=L_{\varphi}=L+{\hat{\rho}}({\varepsilon})+\beta({\varepsilon})\cdot G({\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}L+G)={\OOSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-1}\right)}.$

We define ${\hat{\rho}}({\varepsilon})={\hat{\rho}}\coloneqq 2\cdot\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})$ and by invoking Theorem 0.D.9 we get

$\displaystyle N\geq\frac{{\hat{\rho}}({\varepsilon}){\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{2\mu_{c}^{2}}\beta({\varepsilon})\cdot\log\left(3\beta({\varepsilon})[\Delta_{0}+\beta({\varepsilon}){\mathcal{V}_{0}}]\right)$

as the number of outer iterations. To solve the inner $\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})-$ strongly convex, $L_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})$ -smooth problems with an accuracy of

$\displaystyle{{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2\beta({\varepsilon})}\leq\frac{2\mu_{c}^{2}}{3{\hat{\rho}}({\varepsilon}){\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{2\beta({\varepsilon})}=\frac{\mu_{c}^{2}}{6{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}\cdot\frac{{\varepsilon}}{\beta({\varepsilon})\cdot\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})}={\OOSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{3}\right)}$

and consequently concludes the claim. ∎

Under the assumptions of Theorem 0.D.8 running Algorithm 2 with the quadratic penalty ${{\mathcal{L}}(\,\cdot\,)}\coloneqq{[\,\cdot\,]_{+}^{2}}$ , $\beta({\varepsilon})\coloneqq({\nu^{*}}+1)\frac{{\nu^{*}}+\sqrt{{\nu^{*}}^{2}+2}}{{\varepsilon}}$ , and ${\hat{\rho}}({\varepsilon})\coloneqq 2\cdot\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})$ yields the guarantee

$\displaystyle\left\lvert F_{1}({\theta}^{(N)})-{F^{*}}\right\rvert\leq{\varepsilon}\quad\text{and}\quad[F_{2}({\theta}^{(N)})]_{+}\leq{\varepsilon}.$

$\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{tot}}^{\mathrm{{\mathrm{n}\text{-}sm}}}\geq{\OOTildeSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-2}\right)}\cdot{\OOSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-4}\right)}={\OOTildeSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-6}\right)},$

$\displaystyle\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})=\beta({\varepsilon})\cdot G{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}\cdot\min\{\rho,L\}={\OOSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-1}\right)},$

and has $G_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})$ bounded sub-gradients, where

$\displaystyle G_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})\coloneqq G+{\hat{\rho}}({\varepsilon})\cdot{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}+2\beta({\varepsilon})\cdot{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}G^{2}={\OOSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-1}\right)}.$

We define ${\hat{\rho}}({\varepsilon})\coloneqq 2\cdot\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})$ and by invoking Theorem 0.D.9 we get

$\displaystyle N\geq\frac{{\hat{\rho}}({\varepsilon}){\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{2\mu_{c}^{2}}\beta({\varepsilon})\cdot\log\left(3\beta({\varepsilon})[\Delta_{0}+\beta({\varepsilon}){\mathcal{V}_{0}}]\right)={\OOTildeSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-2}\right)}$

as the number of outer iterations. To solve the inner $\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})-$ strongly convex problems with an accuracy of

we use the sub-gradient method (SM), resulting in an inner complexity of

$\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{in}}^{\mathrm{SM}}({{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}})$ $\displaystyle\geq\frac{4G_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})^{2}}{\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})\cdot{{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}}\geq\frac{4G_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})^{2}}{\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})}\frac{6{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{\mu_{c}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\beta({\varepsilon})\cdot\rho_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})}{{\varepsilon}}$ $\displaystyle=\frac{24{\mathcal{D}^{2}_{\Lambda}}}{\mu_{c}^{2}}\cdot\frac{G_{\varphi}({\varepsilon})^{2}\beta({\varepsilon})}{{\varepsilon}}={\OOSym\!\left({\varepsilon}^{-4}\right)},$

A direct comparison between Corollary 0.D.13 and Corollary 0.D.11 reveals that, in the non-smooth case, the exact penalty function is the natural choice, leading to improved numerical constants since $[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}$ preserves the weak convexity and the upper bound on the sub-gradients without introducing additional constants.

In contrast, in the smooth case, the quadratic penalty function ${[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}^{2}}$ has to be used to smooth the penalty term. While this approach ensures differentiability, it slightly worsens the convergence guarantee compared the results in Fatkhullin et al. [45] .

0.D.4 PPPM under Hidden Strong Convexity

In the case of hidden strong convexity, we can significantly strengthen the result of Theorem 0.D.6 to a logarithmic sample complexity in ${\varepsilon}^{-1}$ for the number of outer loops.

Assume ( HC ) is hidden strongly convex with modulus $\mu_{H}>0$ and we have access to an oracle ${\mathcal{A}}$ according to Assumption 0.D.5 . For a target precision ${\varepsilon}>0$ , lifting parameter ${\hat{\rho}}>\rho$ , and a penalty parameter $\beta\geq 1$ . With the choice of $\alpha\coloneqq\frac{\mu_{c}^{2}\mu_{H}}{{\hat{\rho}}+\mu_{c}^{2}\mu_{H}}\in[0,1]$ and for $N\in{\mathbb{N}}$ , the last iterate of Algorithm 2 satisfies

$\displaystyle P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{{\mathcal{L}},\beta}({\theta}^{(N)})=F_{1}({\theta}^{(N)})-{F^{*}}+\beta{{\mathcal{L}}(F_{2}({\theta}^{(N)}))}\leq{\varepsilon}$

after

$\displaystyle N\geq\frac{{\hat{\rho}}+\mu_{c}^{2}\mu_{H}}{\mu_{c}^{2}\mu_{H}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{2(\Delta_{0}+\beta{\mathcal{V}_{0}})}{{\varepsilon}}\right)$

iterations, where $\Delta_{0}\coloneqq F_{1}(\hat{{\theta}}^{(k)})-{F^{*}}$ and ${\mathcal{V}_{0}}\coloneqq{{\mathcal{L}}(F_{2}({\theta}^{(0)}))}$ . With an inner precision of ${{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}\leq\frac{\alpha{\varepsilon}}{2}$ , the total rate is in general given by

$\displaystyle{T_{\mathrm{tot}}}\geq N\cdot{T_{\mathrm{in}}}({{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}})=\frac{{\hat{\rho}}+\mu_{c}^{2}\mu_{H}}{\mu_{c}^{2}\mu_{H}}\cdot\log\left(\frac{2(\Delta_{0}+\beta{\mathcal{V}_{0}})}{{\varepsilon}}\right)\cdot{T_{\mathrm{in}}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\alpha{\varepsilon}\right).$

Similar to the proof of Theorem 5 in Fatkhullin et al. [45] , we can pick $\alpha$ uniformly small to obtain a recursion of the form

$\displaystyle F_{1}({\theta}^{(k+1)})-{F^{*}}+\beta{{\mathcal{L}}(F_{2}({\theta}^{(k+1)}))}\leq(1-\alpha)(F_{1}({\theta}^{(k)})-{F^{*}}+\beta{{\mathcal{L}}(F_{2}({\theta}^{(k)}))})+{{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}.$

By unrolling, we obtain

$\displaystyle P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{{\mathcal{L}},\beta}({\theta}^{(N)})=F_{1}({\theta}^{(N)})-{F^{*}}+\beta{{\mathcal{L}}(F_{2}({\theta}^{(N)}))}\leq(1-\alpha)^{N}(\Delta_{0}+\beta)+\frac{{{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}}{\alpha}.$

The claim follows by the choice of $\alpha,{{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{in}}}$ and $N$ . ∎

0.D.5 Technical Lemmas

For our penalty approach, we are generally interested in weak convexity of the penalty term, a (generalized) chain rule as well as bounded sub-gradients and $L$ -smoothness respectively. For the chain rule in the smooth case, we use the result of Drusvyatskiy and Paquette [40] , and in the non-smooth case we generalize their results to both, the special case of the quadratic penalty ${[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}^{2}}$ and the exact penalty $[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}$ .

Before we start analyzing the two cases separately, we state a few utility Lemmas.

Let $h:{\mathcal{Y}}\to{\mathbb{R}},{\tilde{\theta}}\mapsto{[{\tilde{\theta}}]_{+}^{2}}$ , ${\mathcal{Y}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m}$ , $m\in{\mathbb{N}}$ be the quadratic penalty function over a ${\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}}$ -bounded domain. Then $h$ is $2\cdot{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}}$ -Lipschitz continuous.

Furthermore, if composed with a $G_{f}$ -Lipschitz continuous function $f:{\Theta}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}$ , over a ${\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}$ -bounded, compact domain ${\Theta}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ , the resulting function $h\circ f$ is $2\cdot{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}\cdot G_{f}^{2}$ -Lipschitz continuous.

We first consider only the quadratic norm. Since we assume to have a feasible point for our problem ( HC ), we can assume that ${\mathcal{Y}}$ contains the origin. More generally, one would obtain $2\sup_{{v}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\lVert[{v}]_{+}\rVert$ as a Lipschitz constant.

The penalty function is globally $2\cdot{\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{Y}}}$ -Lipschitz continuous. I.e. for ${v},{w}\in\Image(f)$ we have

$\displaystyle\left\lvert h({v})-h({w})\right\rvert=\left\lvert\lVert{v}\rVert^{2}-\lVert{w}\rVert^{2}\right\rvert=\left\lvert\langle{v}+{w},{v}-{w}\rangle\right\rvert\leq\left(\lVert{v}\rVert+\lVert{w}\rVert\right)\cdot\lVert{v}-{w}\rVert,$ (12)

which implies $2\cdot{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}$ -Lipschitz continuity on a bounded domain. The same also holds true if we substitute $\lVert\,\cdot\,{}\rVert^{2}$ by ${[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}^{2}}$ .

Since $f$ is Lipschitz continuous and ${\Theta}$ is compact and bounded, also the image ${\mathcal{Y}}\coloneqq\Image(f)\coloneqq f({\Theta})$ is compact and bounded by $G_{f}\cdot{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}$ . Invoking ( 12 ) with ${v}=f({\theta}),{w}=f({\tilde{\theta}})$ , ${\theta},{\tilde{\theta}}\in{\Theta}$ , yields that $h\circ f$ is $(2\cdot G_{f}\cdot{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}\cdot G_{f})$ -Lipschitz continuous, concluding the claim. ∎

Let $f:{\Theta}\to{\mathbb{R}}$ , with ${\Theta}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ bounded and convex, be $\rho$ -weakly convex with a bounded range, and let $h:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}$ be non-decreasing, convex and $G_{h}$ -Lipschitz continuous on $\range(f)$ . Then the composition $g\coloneqq h\circ f:{\Theta}\to{\mathbb{R}}$ is $(G_{h}\cdot\rho)$ -weakly convex.

To show $\mu$ -weak convexity, we need to prove that for all ${\theta},{\tilde{\theta}}\in{\Theta}$ and all $t\in[0,1]$ the inequality

$\displaystyle g(t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}})\leq tg({\theta})+(1-t)g({\tilde{\theta}})+\frac{\mu}{2}t(1-t)\lVert{\theta}-{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}$

holds. Since by assumption $f$ is $\rho$ -WC, we know that the function ${\tilde{f}}({\theta})\coloneqq f({\theta})+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}$ is convex. Let ${\theta},{\tilde{\theta}}\in{\Theta}$ and $t\in[0,1]$ be arbitrary but fixed. By convexity of ${\tilde{f}}$ , we obtain

$\displaystyle f(t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}})+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}\leq t\left(f({\theta})+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}\right)+(1-t)\left(f({\tilde{\theta}})+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}\right).$

Since the Euclidean norm is $2$ -strongly convex, we have

$\displaystyle\lVert t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}=t\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}+(1-t)\lVert{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}-t(1-t)\lVert{\theta}-{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}.$

and thus the left-hand side expands to

$\displaystyle f(t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}})+\frac{\rho}{2}\lVert t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}$ $\displaystyle\quad=f(t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}})+\frac{\rho}{2}\left[t\lVert{\theta}\rVert^{2}+(1-t)\lVert{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}-t(1-t)\lVert{\theta}-{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}\right].$

Rearranging yields

$\displaystyle f(t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}})\leq tf({\theta})+(1-t)f({\tilde{\theta}})+\underbrace{\frac{\rho}{2}(1-t)t\lVert{\theta}-{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}}_{\eqqcolon\delta\geq 0}.$

By inserting the above into $h$ we obtain

$\displaystyle g\left(t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}}\right)$ $\displaystyle=h\left(f(t{\theta}+(1-t){\tilde{\theta}})\right)$ $\displaystyle{\leq}h\left(tf({\theta})+(1-t)f({\tilde{\theta}})+\delta\right)$ $\displaystyle{\leq}h\left(tf({\theta})+(1-t)f({\tilde{\theta}})\right)+G_{h}\cdot\delta$ $\displaystyle{\leq}th\left(f({\theta})\right)+(1-t)h\left(f({\tilde{\theta}})\right)+G_{h}\cdot\delta$ $\displaystyle{=}tg({\theta})+(1-t)g({\tilde{\theta}})+\frac{G_{h}\cdot\rho}{2}(1-t)t\lVert{\theta}-{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert^{2}$

by applying in $(i)$ that $h$ is non-decreasing, in $(ii)$ the Lipschitz continuity of $h$ , and in $(iii)$ the convexity of $h$ . This concludes the claim. ∎

0.D.5.1 Smooth Case: Quadratic Penalty

We show that the penalty term is weakly convex, satisfies the chain rule and we give a uniform upper bound for the gradients.

Let ${\Theta}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ be a ${\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}$ -bounded, compact domain, and $h:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}},{\theta}\mapsto{[{\theta}]_{+}^{2}}$ be the quadratic penalty function. We assume that $f:{\Theta}\to{\mathbb{R}}$ is $L$ -smooth, $\rho$ -weakly convex and has $G_{f}$ uniformly bounded gradients. Then the composition $g\coloneqq h\circ f:{\Theta}\to{\mathbb{R}}$ satisfies:

(Weak Convexity) The penalty term $g$ is $(2G_{f}{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}\cdot\min\{\rho,L\})$ -weakly convex.

(Chain Rule) For all ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ we have

$\displaystyle\nabla g({\theta})=h^{\prime}(f({\theta}))\cdot\nabla f({\theta})=2[f({\theta})]_{+}\cdot\nabla f({\theta}).$

(Bounded Gradients) The gradients of $g$ are uniformly bounded by $G_{g}\coloneqq 2{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}G_{f}^{2}$ .

( $L_{g}$ -smoothness) The penalty term $g$ is $L_{g}$ -smooth, with $L_{g}\coloneqq 2({F_{\mathsf{UB}}}L+G_{f}^{2})$ ,

where $\max_{{\theta}\in{\Theta}}\lvert f({\theta})\rvert\eqqcolon{F_{\mathsf{UB}}}<\infty$ is an upper bound.

The weak convexity is a consequence of Lemma 0.D.16 and Lemma 4.2 in Drusvyatskiy and Paquette [40] . We apply Lemma 0.D.15 to obtain that $g$ is Lipschitz continuous. The third claim follows by Theorem 3.1 in Drusvyatskiy and Paquette [40] . To show the $L_{g}$ -smoothness, let ${\theta},{\tilde{\theta}}\in{\Theta}$ . We have

$\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla g({\theta})-\nabla g({\tilde{\theta}})\right\rVert$ $\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\left\lVert[f({\theta})]_{+}\nabla f({\theta})-[f({\theta})]_{+}\nabla f({\tilde{\theta}})+[f({\theta})]_{+}\nabla f({\tilde{\theta}})-[f({\tilde{\theta}})]_{+}\nabla f({\tilde{\theta}})\right\rVert$ $\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\lVert[f({\theta})]_{+}(\nabla f({\theta})-\nabla f({\tilde{\theta}}))\rVert+2\cdot\lVert([f({\theta})]_{+}-[f({\tilde{\theta}})]_{+})\nabla f({\tilde{\theta}})\rVert$ $\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot{F_{\mathsf{UB}}}L\lVert{\theta}-{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert+2\cdot G_{f}G_{f}\lVert{\theta}-{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert$ $\displaystyle=2({F_{\mathsf{UB}}}L+G_{f}^{2})\cdot\lVert{\theta}-{\tilde{\theta}}\rVert,$

since for all ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ we have $[f({\theta})]_{+}\leq\lvert f({\theta})\rvert\leq\max_{{\theta}\in{\Theta}}\lvert f({\theta})\rvert\eqqcolon{F_{\mathsf{UB}}}<\infty$ . This concludes the claim. ∎

0.D.5.2 Non-smooth Case: Exact and Quadratic Penalty

We can generalize the result above to the case of non-smooth $f$ and $h$ being either the quadratic ${[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}^{2}}$ or exact penalty $[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}$ .

Let $f:{\Theta}\to{\mathbb{R}}$ be a $\rho$ -weakly convex, (potentially) non-smooth, $G_{f}$ -Lipschitz continuous function over a ${\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}$ -bounded domain ${\Theta}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ . Furthermore, let $h:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}},{\theta}\mapsto{[{\theta}]_{+}^{2}}$ be the quadratic penalty function. Then the composition $g\coloneqq h\circ f$ satisfies:

(Weak Convexity) The penalty term $g$ is $(2G_{f}{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}\cdot\rho)$ -weakly convex.

(Generalized Chain Rule) For all ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ we have

$\displaystyle\partial g({\theta})=2[f({\theta})]_{+}\cdot\partial f({\theta}).$ (13)

(Bounded Sub-gradients) The sub-gradients of $g$ are uniformly bounded by $G_{g}\coloneqq 2{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}G_{f}^{2}$ .

By Lemma 0.D.15 we know that $h$ is $(2G_{f}{\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}})$ —Lipschitz continuous and the third claim is proved. Consequently, the first claim follows by applying Lemma 0.D.16 .

Since $g$ is weakly convex, it is regular by Proposition 4.4 in Vial [115] , according to Definition 3 in Li et al. [69] of regularity, which is by Corollary 8.11 in Rockafellar and Wets [105] equivalent to Definition 6.24 and Corollary 6.29 in Rockafellar and Wets [105] of regularity. Applying the generalized chain rule for sub-gradients, Theorem 10.49 of Rockafellar and Wets [105] and using the fact that $h$ is continuously differentiable concludes the claim. ∎

Let $f:{\Theta}\to{\mathbb{R}}$ be a $\rho$ -weakly convex, (potentially) non-smooth, $G_{f}$ -Lipschitz continuous function over a ${\mathcal{D}_{\Theta}}$ -bounded domain ${\Theta}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}$ . Furthermore, let $h:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}},{\theta}\mapsto[{\theta}]_{+}$ be the exact penalty function. Then the composition $g\coloneqq h\circ f$ satisfies:

(Weak Convexity) The penalty term $g$ is $\rho$ -weakly convex.

(Generalized Chain Rule) For all ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ we have

$\displaystyle\partial g({\theta})=\begin{cases}\partial f({\theta}),&f({\theta})>0,\\ [0,1]\cdot\partial f({\theta}),&f({\theta})=0,\\ \{0\},&f({\theta})<0,\end{cases}$ (14)

with a Minkowski product in the second case.

(Bounded Sub-gradients) The sub-gradients of $g$ are uniformly bounded by $G_{g}\coloneqq G_{f}$ .

If $f$ is $G_{f}$ -Lipschitz, then trivially also $h\circ f$ is $G_{f}$ -Lipschitz. Additionally, $[\,\cdot\,{}]_{+}$ is non-decreasing, convex, and $1$ -Lipschitz. Thus, the first claim follows by applying Lemma 0.D.16 . With the same argument as above, we can apply the generalized chain rule for sub-gradients, Thm. 10.49 in Rockafellar and Wets [105] , and obtain

$\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}g({\theta})=\bigcup_{y\in\partial h(f({\theta}))}\partial(yf)({\theta}),$

with ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ . To obtain ( 14 ), we use the known sub-differential of $[{\theta}]_{+}$ :

$\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}[{\theta}]_{+}=\begin{cases}\{1\},&{\theta}>0,\\ [0,1],&{\theta}=0,\\ \{0\},&{\theta}<0.\end{cases}$

Thus, at $f({\theta})=0$ , we obtain a convex combination of sub-gradients $g\in\partial f({\theta})$ and weights $\alpha\in[0,1]$ . The result follows. ∎

0.D.6 Strong Duality: Translation of Convergence Guarantees

The challenge in Section 5.1 is the translation of the guarantee on ${\mathbb{E}}\!\left[P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{{\mathcal{L}},\beta}(\,\cdot\,{})\right]$ to a guarantee on the constraint violation, namely ${\mathbb{E}}\!\left[[F_{2}(\,\cdot\,{})]_{+}\right]$ . The following results show how to perform this translation under strong duality for both, the exact and the quadratic penalty function. For simplicity, we formulate the following corollaries in the deterministic case. Taking expectation yields the desired results.

Let ${\theta}^{*}$ be the optimal solution of ( CME ) and assume that strong duality holds, i.e. there exists ${\nu^{*}}\geq 0$ such that ${F^{*}}=d({\nu^{*}})$ . Then we have for all ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ the inequality

$\displaystyle-{\nu^{*}}[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}\leq F_{1}({\theta})-{F^{*}}.$

In particular, for all ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ and any $\beta\geq 2\cdot{\nu^{*}}$ , we have

$\displaystyle 0$ $\displaystyle\leq F_{1}({\theta})-{F^{*}}+\beta[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}=P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{1,\beta}({\theta}).$

Lemma 3.1.21 in Nesterov [95] . ∎

Assume that for ( CME ) strong duality holds with a Lagrangian multiplier ${\nu^{*}}\geq 0$ , and let ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ be a point such that ${\mathbb{E}}\!\left[P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{1,\beta}({\theta})\right]\leq{\varepsilon}$ with the exact penalty and $\beta>{\nu^{*}}$ . Then we have the following translation for the constraint violation:

$\displaystyle 0$ $\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}\right]\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{1,\beta}({\theta})\right]}{\beta-{\nu^{*}}}\leq\frac{{\varepsilon}}{\beta-{\nu^{*}}}.$

We invoke Lemma 0.D.20 and obtain as a trivial consequence:

$\displaystyle-{\nu^{*}}[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}\leq F_{1}({\theta})-{F^{*}}+\beta[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}-\beta[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}.$

Taking expectation concludes the claim. ∎

Assume that for ( CME ) strong duality holds with a Lagrangian multiplier ${\nu^{*}}\geq 0$ , and let ${\theta}\in{\Theta}$ be a point such that ${\mathbb{E}}\!\left[P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{2,\beta}({\theta})\right]\leq{\varepsilon}$ with the quadratic penalty. Then we have the following translation for the constraint violation:

$\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}\right]\leq\frac{{\nu^{*}}+\sqrt{{\nu^{*}}^{2}+2\beta{\varepsilon}}}{\beta}.$

We invoke Lemma 0.D.20 and obtain

$\displaystyle{\varepsilon}\geq{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[P^{\mathsf{opt}}_{2,\beta}({\theta})\right]={\mathbb{E}}\!\left[F_{1}({\theta})-{F^{*}}+\beta{[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}^{2}}\right]\overset{\text{Jensen}}{\geq}-{\nu^{*}}{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}\right]+\beta\left({\mathbb{E}}\!\left[[F_{2}({\theta})]_{+}\right]\right)^{2}.$

Solving

$\displaystyle s\left(\beta\cdot s-{\nu^{*}}\right)-{\varepsilon}\leq 0,\;\text{s.t.}\;s\geq 0$

concludes the claim. ∎

0.E.3 Continuous State-Action Space

We first discuss details on the estimation of the state-action occupancy measure and then provide details on the PointMass and the SafeCartpole experiment.

0.E.3.1 Occupancy Measure Estimate

When scaling to continuous state-action spaces in constrained maximum entropy exploration, or more generally in convex RL, reliably estimating the occupancy measure ( λ MC H -Est ) is the main bottleneck. In our case, we use and implement the state-of-the-art technique proposed by Barakat et al. [12] relying on Maximum Likelihood Estimators (MLE) for learning the approximation ${\widehat{\lambda}}$ compared to the Mean Squared Error, which previous techniques have been proposing. Compared to our formulation of $\lambda^{\pi}:{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\to[0,1]$ as a function of the state and action space, Barakat et al. [12] rewrite $\lambda^{\pi}$ of a policy $\pi$ in the form

$\displaystyle\lambda^{\pi}({s},{a})\coloneqq d^{\pi}({s})\cdot\pi({a}|{s}),$

and only approximate the state-occupancy measure $d^{\pi}$ with a function approximator.

In particular, at every step of the algorithm, we collect a batch of $N_{{\widehat{\lambda}}}\in{\mathbb{N}}$ samples $B_{{\widehat{\lambda}}}\coloneqq\{{s}_{i},{a}_{i}\}_{i\in[N_{{\widehat{\lambda}}}]}$ , consisting of potentially several rollouts and then approximate $d^{\pi}$ with a parametric class of probability distributions

$\displaystyle\mathrm{GMM}(K)\coloneqq\left\{p_{\textbf{w}}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_{k},\Sigma_{k}):{\textbf{w}}=(w_{k},\mathbf{m}_{k},\Sigma_{k})_{k\in[K]}\right.$ $\displaystyle\left.\;\mathrm{s.t.}\;w_{k}\geq 0,\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}=1,\mathbf{m}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}},\Sigma_{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{s}}_{++},\right\}$

in our case Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs), with $K=16$ . The approximation $\hat{d}^{\pi}$ is learned by maximizing the MLE loss, or equivalently minimizing a Kullback Leibler divergence [ 108 ] , i.e.

$\displaystyle\hat{d}^{\pi}\coloneqq p_{{\textbf{w}}^{*}},\quad p_{{\textbf{w}}^{*}}\in\argmax_{p_{\textbf{w}}\in\mathrm{GMM}(K)}\frac{1}{N_{{\widehat{\lambda}}}}\sum_{i\in[N_{{\widehat{\lambda}}}]}\log p_{\textbf{w}}({s}_{i}).$ ( $\hat{d}^{\pi}$ -Est)

Following Barakat et al. [12] , the estimate ${\widehat{\lambda}}^{\pi}$ is then given by

$\displaystyle{\widehat{\lambda}}^{\pi}({s},{a})\coloneqq\hat{d}^{\pi}({s})\cdot\pi({a}|{s}).$ ( $\lambda^{\mathrm{MLE}}_{H}$ -Est)

0.E.3.2 Imitation Learning Constraint: PointMass Exploration

We provide additional implementation and computational details on the PointMass experiment.

State, Action & Observation Space.

In the PointMass environment, the state, action and observation space are

$\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times\Obs={\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.$

At timestep $t$ , each observation $o_{t}$ consists of the current $x$ and $y$ coordinate of the point mass. The action ${a}$ encodes the gain along the $x$ and $y$ -axis respectively.

Objective and Constraint: Definition & Implementation.

First, we train a reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ using the Soft-Actor critic (SAC) algorithm of the StableBaselines3 package [ 101 ] . We fit the reference GMM from stochastic SAC rollouts using the same discounted weighted likelihood as in ( ^ d π -Est ). On a batch $B$ , the apprenticeship constraint and its pseudo-reward are

$\displaystyle\widehat{{\mathcal{R}}}_{\rm KL}$ $\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i\in B}\bar{w}_{i}\left(\log{\widehat{\lambda}}^{\pi}({s}_{i},{a}_{i})-\log{\widehat{\lambda}}^{\rm ref}({s}_{i},{a}_{i})\right)-{\mathcal{R}_{\max}},$ $\displaystyle r_{\rm KL}({s}_{i},{a}_{i})$ $\displaystyle\coloneqq 1+\log{\widehat{\lambda}}^{\pi}({s}_{i},{a}_{i})-\log{\widehat{\lambda}}^{\rm ref}({s}_{i},{a}_{i}).$

The budget ${\mathcal{R}_{\max}}$ affects the constraint value but, being constant, does not enter its pseudo-reward.

Analysis of Actions.

Figure 14 shows that as the constraint budget ${\mathcal{R}_{\max}}$ increases, the actions of $\pi_{\theta}$ deviate progressively further from those of $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ . This directly reflects the difficulty of the task: the SAC reference policy consistently directs the agent toward the goal $(0,0)$ , so effective exploration requires substantial deviation from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ .

Colored arrows indicate the actions selected by each policy at the current state. For PGP policies, the corresponding reference action $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\,\cdot\,{}\mid{s})$ is overlaid in gray, illustrating the increasing deviation from the reference as ${\mathcal{R}_{\max}}$ grows.

0.E.3.3 Safety Constrained: Cartpole Exploration

We provide implementation and computational details on the SafeCartpole experiment.

State, Action & Observation Space.

In the SafeCartpole environment, the state, action and observation space are

$\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times\Obs={\mathbb{R}}^{4}\times{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{5},$ (15)

where each observation $o_{t}$ at timestep $t\in[0,{T_{\max}}]$ consists of

$\displaystyle o_{t}=[x_{t},\sin(\omega_{t}),\cos(\omega_{t}),\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\>\mathrm{d}t}x_{t},\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\>\mathrm{d}t}\omega_{t}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{5},$ ( $o_{t}$ -Cartpole)

and $\omega_{t}$ is the current angle of the pole, $x_{t}$ the slider’s position with its corresponding velocity, and the last component encodes the angular velocity of the pole via the corresponding time derivatives of the angular observations. The state consists of

$\displaystyle{s}_{t}=[x_{t},\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\>\mathrm{d}t}x_{t},\omega_{t},\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\>\mathrm{d}t}\omega_{t}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4},$

i.e. includes not only the position and the angle, but also their corresponding derivatives. Thus, effective exploration of the state action space requires covering the position and velocity of the cart as well as the angles of the pole, and also entails the angular velocities of the pole, making the task significantly harder for constrained maximum entropy. An action ${a}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}$ is a real-valued action which is passed through a motor gear and then acts on the system.

Objective and Constraint: Definition & Implementation Details.

After the state-action occupancy measure is estimated via the MLE in ( ^ d π -Est ), we can estimate the entropy objective and the constraint.

The constraint function is given by

$\displaystyle c({s}_{t},{a}_{t})\coloneqq\max(\lvert x_{t}\rvert-2.0,0),$

where $x_{t}$ encodes the slider’s position at time $t\in[0,{T_{\max}}]$ . For the sake of presentation, we also log the values $\lvert x_{t}\rvert-2.0$ to make the constraint violation plot more expressive. The entropy and the cost function can be then computed by point-wise multiplications of the tensors given by: ${\widehat{\lambda}}$ evaluated on the state and action pairs, the logarithm of ${\widehat{\lambda}}$ for the objective and the constraint function values $(c({s}_{t},{a}_{t}))_{t}$ for each rollout, respectively. For numerical stability, we perform a log-sum-exp rescaling for these tensor computations. Afterwards we apply the squared penalty, sum both terms and obtain the pseudo-reward for which we compute the gradient using PyTorch’s [ 98 ] automatic differentiation framework. The resulting gradient is plugged in the ( PG-Thm ) to compute the cumulative reward and optimize. We use a critic network as a baseline for the REINFORCE algorithm to improve stability, i.e. ( PG-Thm ) acts on the advantage function.

Detailed Analysis of the Results.

We analyze separately:

Entropy and Constraint Violation. In Figure 4(a) we observe a significant jump at $N=1000$ in the entropy ${\mathcal{H}}$ which, combined with Figure 17 and additional logging information, corresponds to the policy understanding that symmetric discovery drastically improves the exploration in the state-action space. Afterwards, we observe a slight drop in the entropy again, as the policy needs to relearn, or more precisely combine, to turn the pole upwards and discover the space in a symmetric way. As evident in Figure 16 (bottom left), later episodes then learn how to push the penalty function right below the threshold to accelerate faster, i.e. get up the pole quicker and then discover even more. The final policy has almost perfect symmetry over the rollout horizon with varying angular velocities in the lower half of the angular part of the state space.

Controls. Figure 16 shows that in particular early controls are slightly fuzzy and exhibit high(er) variance. In later iterations, i.e. $k\in[1000,2500]\cap{\mathbb{N}}$ , the policy $\pi^{(k)}$ correctly learns a bang-bang control style, which is optimal to turn the pole upward, especially since we do not penalize actions in our setting. This behavior is for larger $k$ refined and the final policy perfectly flips the pole to the upper half of the angular’s state space and is confirmed as the correct behavior by the reduced variance $\sigma^{2}_{\theta}$ , as it also improves on the tiny constraint violation at the end of the observation horizon.
